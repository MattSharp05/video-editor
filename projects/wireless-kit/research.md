# Caught by Wireless (Crippen case, 1910): research (fresh research agent, 2026-11-08)

## Crippen 1910 fact base for a 3-part Reel series

**Recommendation (A).** I checked Crippen against two other cases.
- **Stratton brothers, Deptford, 1905.** This was Britain's first murder conviction on fingerprint evidence. It is solved and well documented, but the crime itself was violent, there is no chase, no twist, and the visuals are thin.
- **"Brides in the Bath", G. J. Smith, 1915.** Spilsbury's forensics carry the story, but you can't tell it without explaining how the women died.

Crippen beats both. It is solved in law (Old Bailey verdict, 1910) and everyone involved is long dead (Ethel Le Neve died in 1967). The primary sources are strong: the full trial transcript, plus 1910 newspapers that followed the chase day by day. The story falls into three acts without effort: the disappearance, the chase at sea, then the arrest and trial. It is very visual: a disguise, two liners racing, wireless sparks, newspapers. And it has a real modern twist, the DNA dispute. You can tell it without gore, because the remains never need to be described. **Recommendation: Crippen.**

### 1. Timeline (Old Bailey transcript unless marked)
- **19 Jan 1910:** Crippen collects 5 grains of hyoscine hydrobromide from Lewis & Burrows, 108 New Oxford Street. He ordered it on 17–18 Jan and signed the poisons book "Munyons, per H. H. Crippen … for homeopathic preparations".
- **31 Jan – about 1:30 am 1 Feb:** Paul and Clara Martinetti have dinner and play whist at 39 Hilldrop Crescent. Clara: "Mrs. Crippen came to the top of the steps and wished me good-bye… I never saw her again." The trial says "dinner", not a party. On 1 Feb Crippen tells Clara "She is all right."
- **Early Feb:** friends hear that Cora (stage name Belle Elmore) has "gone to America". Crippen says a cable called her away and that she was going "straight on to California".
- **20 Feb:** Ethel is seen at the Music Hall Benevolent Fund ball wearing Cora's brooch. The defence conceded that Ethel "wore jewellery and furs which once belonged to Mrs. Crippen".
- **Late March:** Crippen tells people Cora is very ill (23 Mar). He then sends a telegram, "Belle died yesterday", and a death notice appears in *The Era* on 26 Mar. He says she died "in Los Angeles", of pleuro-pneumonia, and was cremated. He takes Ethel to Dieppe at Easter. The friends grow suspicious: they ask for addresses, sailings and wreath details and get vague answers.
- **8 Jul:** Chief Inspector Walter Dew interviews Crippen at Albion House. Crippen: "I suppose I had better tell the truth. The stories I have told them about her death are untrue; as far as I know she is still alive." In his signed statement he says she left him and he believed she had gone to Chicago to the performer Bruce Miller. He then shows Dew round the house, cellar included.
- **9 Jul:** Crippen settles his debts, cashes a cheque and writes "in order to escape trouble I shall be obliged to absent myself for a time". He and Ethel leave: he shaves his moustache and stops wearing glasses in public, and she poses as his son. They go to Brussels, then Antwerp, as "Mr. and Master Robinson".
- **11 Jul:** Dew finds the house empty, searches it and circulates descriptions of the pair to ports.
- **12–13 Jul:** On the 13th Dew probes the cellar's brick floor with a poker. On air: *police found human remains buried under the cellar floor.*
- **16 Jul:** a warrant is issued.
- **20 Jul:** the pair sail from Antwerp on the Canadian Pacific liner *Montrose*, booked as "John Robinson, 55, merchant" and "John C. Robinson, 16, student".
- **On board:** Captain Henry Kendall grows suspicious. The press reported he first suspected them about two hours out of Antwerp (*Montreal Star* item reprinted in US papers, 29 Jul 1910). The squeezed hands, ill-fitting clothes and hat stuffed to fit come from Kendall's own later accounts, retold by Larson. I could not verify those against a primary source.
- **About 22 Jul (some sources say 21 Jul):** Kendall has his wireless operator, Lawrence Hughes, send a message to the shipping line's office in Liverpool, which passes it to Scotland Yard. The ship was roughly 120–130 miles west of Cornwall, near the edge of wireless range. The usually quoted text is: "Have strong suspicions that Crippen London cellar murderer and accomplice are among saloon passengers. Moustache taken off growing beard. Accomplice dressed as boy. Manner and build undoubtedly a girl." **I could not verify this exact wording against a primary document.** It comes from Wikipedia and Larson-based retellings. A Washington Herald report of 3 Aug 1910 paraphrases Kendall: "every reason to believe Crippen and Le Neve aboard… latter disguised [as a] boy." Treat it as the commonly quoted version and show it only in shortened form.
- **23 Jul:** Dew sails from Liverpool on the faster White Star liner *Laurentic*. Some accounts say he boarded on 22 Jul.
- **29 Jul:** the *Laurentic* reaches Father Point (Pointe-au-Père), near Rimouski, Quebec. Dew waits there. Wireless messages flew all night (Washington Herald, 30 Jul 1910).
- **The newspaper frenzy:** papers printed Kendall's wireless reports and day-by-day charts of the race. The Montrose had no news service for passengers, so Crippen could not follow any of it. Safe wording: "the whole world was reading about the chase. Crippen had no idea."
- **31 Jul:** Dew boards the *Montrose* at Father Point, dressed as a pilot, and arrests Crippen in the captain's cabin; Canadian police cautioned him. Ethel is found in another cabin "dressed as a boy, with her hair cut short". Crippen testified that Dew said "Good morning, Dr. Crippen, I am Inspector Dew" and that he replied "Good morning, Mr. Dew." The popular version, from Dew's memoir, is "Good morning, Dr. Crippen. Do you know me? I'm Chief Inspector Dew from Scotland Yard", with Crippen's reply "Thank God it's over. The suspense has been too great." The two versions differ.
- **28 Aug:** the pair arrive back in Liverpool on the *Megantic*.
- **Trial:** Old Bailey, 18–22 Oct 1910, before the Lord Chief Justice (Lord Alverstone). The case rested on:
  - **The poison purchase.** The Home Office analyst found hyoscine in the remains. Say only that "a scientist found traces of a rare drug Crippen had bought."
  - **The pyjama jacket.** A piece found with the remains carried the label "Jones Brothers (Holloway), Limited". Crippen admitted he bought his pyjamas at Jones Brothers in September 1909, and a pair of pyjama trousers in the house had no matching jacket.
  - **The scar.** Cora's family testified she had an abdominal scar, and the prosecution said a matching mark was on the remains. On air, say only that "experts argued about a scar."
- **Verdict:** "Guilty of wilful murder. Sentence, Death." An appeal was dismissed in early November.
- **Execution:** **23 Nov 1910, Pentonville Prison.**
- **Ethel Le Neve:** tried separately as an accessory after the fact on 25 Oct 1910 and **acquitted**.

### 2. The twist (DNA)
- **2007 announcement, published as Foran et al., J Forensic Sci 56(1):233–240.** Published online 2010, print Jan 2011. Michigan State's David Foran tested tissue on the microscope slide that the pathologist Bernard Spilsbury had prepared for the 1910 trial, held by the Royal London Hospital archives. They compared its mitochondrial DNA with three living great-nieces of Cora, traced by genealogist Beth Wills: no match. A later Y-chromosome test said the tissue was **male**.
- **Why it's disputed:**
  - Genealogist Jonathan Menges argued the family tree linking those relatives to Cora is unproven.
  - The male finding was first shown on TV (PBS *Secrets of the Dead*) before peer review.
  - It rests on a single century-old, formalin-fixed slide, using a very new technique (David Aaronovitch's criticism).
  - Contamination over a century of handling, and whether the slide really holds the cellar tissue, have both been raised. I found no peer-reviewed rebuttal of the lab work itself.
  - In Dec 2009 the Criminal Cases Review Commission declined to send the case back to the Court of Appeal, saying the applicant was not a "properly interested person".
  - Historians (Connell, Larson) still regard Crippen as guilty. I did not open their books.
- **Safe on-air wording:** "In 2007, scientists tested a tiny sample kept from the trial. They said it didn't match Cora's family. Other experts say the test may be wrong. A jury said Crippen was guilty, and the case has never been reopened. The mystery still has people arguing."
- **Never say:** "DNA proved Crippen innocent", "the body was a man", or "Cora was never found dead" as facts.

### 3. "Firsts"
The common claim is that Crippen was "the first criminal captured with the aid of wireless" (Wikipedia, citing PBS; repeated widely). An earlier "first by telecommunication" exists: John Tawell, 1845, caught by the electric wired telegraph. I found no documented earlier wireless capture, but no authoritative source rules one out either.
- **Safe wording:** "one of the first times ever, and often called the very first, that a radio message from a ship helped catch a suspect."
- **Avoid:** "the first criminal ever caught by telegraph."

### 4. Visual details
- **39 Hilldrop Crescent:** a semi-detached Victorian house in Holloway, north London, with front steps (Clara's goodbye was at "the top of the steps"), an upstairs parlour, a coal cellar with a brick floor, and a garden. The house no longer stands.
- **Crippen:** American, born in 1862 in Coldwater, Michigan; aged 48 at the trial (listed as "dentist"). Small, gold-rimmed glasses, a heavy moustache (shaved off for the flight, clean-shaven by the arrest). He sold patent medicines (Munyon's).
- **Ethel:** his typist. On the ship: boy's suit, short hair, a "student" aged 16.
- **The ships:** the *Montrose* was a Canadian Pacific single-funnel liner. The *Laurentic* was a newer White Star liner, faster and also single-funnelled.
- **Father Point:** a lighthouse and pilot station on the St Lawrence, near Rimouski. The pilot boat took Dew out to the ship.
- **The wireless room:** a Marconi set with sparks, earphones and a Morse key, run by Lawrence Hughes.
- **Dew:** a heavy-set Chief Inspector, dressed in a pilot's cap and coat.
- **Newspapers:** 1910 front pages with ship-race maps and "Robinson" headlines.

### 5. Where sources disagree, and safe language
- **Disagreements:**
  - The date of Kendall's message (21 vs 22 Jul) and who received it (the line's Liverpool office vs Scotland Yard).
  - Its exact text.
  - Dew's words at the arrest (trial vs memoir).
  - "Party" vs dinner and whist.
  - Dew boarding the Laurentic on 22 vs 23 Jul.
  - Who first suspected the pair: Kendall, or Third Officer Mowatt, who claimed the credit (1 Aug 1910 papers).
  - The DNA result.
- **False or too graphic on air:**
  - Anything about the condition of the remains. Note that Dew's own arrest wording included "mutilation". Don't quote it.
  - How hyoscine works.
  - "Body parts".
  - Details of the hanging.
  - "DNA freed him".
  - "Ethel helped kill her". She was acquitted.
- **Gentle and true wordings:**
  - "Cora vanished, and police found human remains under the cellar floor."
  - "Crippen was charged with murdering his wife."
  - "The jury found him guilty of murder. He was executed in November 1910." (An option for "hanged": "he was put to death, which was the law back then.")
  - "Ethel was found not guilty and went free."

### 6. Cliffhangers and hooks (each true as worded)
**Part 1 endings:**
1. "On 13 July, Inspector Dew tapped the cellar floor, and one brick came loose."
2. "Dew came back to ask more questions. But Crippen and Ethel were gone."
3. "Police found human remains under the cellar floor. And the doctor had vanished."

**Part 2 endings:**
1. "The captain's secret message flew out across the sea, just before his ship sailed out of radio range."
2. "A faster ship was racing to get there first. Crippen had no idea."
3. "At dawn, a pilot climbed aboard. But he wasn't really a pilot."

**Hooks:**
1. "In 1910 a woman vanished, and a radio message helped catch the man accused of her murder."
2. "Why did a ship's captain think a 16-year-old boy was really a girl in disguise?"
3. "He was found guilty in 1910. A hundred years later, a DNA test made people argue all over again."
