## Volcano (`volcano-01`)
Why does one volcano ooze while another blows its top off? 🌋 It's the gas.

Magma holds gas like the fizz in a soda. Runny magma, like Kilauea's in Hawaii, lets the bubbles escape, so lava pours
out in glowing rivers. Sticky, silica-rich magma, like Mount St. Helens', traps them until they burst.

On May 18, 1980, an earthquake set off the largest landslide in recorded history on St. Helens' bulging north side.
The trapped gas blasted out, and ash rose about 15 miles (24 km) in under 15 minutes (USGS).

Real terrain: USGS 3DEP elevation and USGS National Map imagery (public domain). St. Helens' pre-1980 summit is
reconstructed, and heights are exaggerated where marked.

## Vegas Sphere (`vegas-sphere-01`)
Is the Sphere in Las Vegas just one giant TV? 📺 Not quite.

It's about 1.2 million hockey-puck-shaped lights on a steel frame, about 8 inches apart. Each puck is one dot of the
picture, and a computer tells every one what color to show, many times a second. Up close you'd see dots and dark gaps.
From far away, your eyes blend them into one picture. Inside is a second, much sharper screen that wraps over your head.

Simplified illustration: the computer, wiring and interior are drawn to explain them, not to scale. The puck count is
approximate.

## Octopus (`octopus-01`)
Look twice: that rock is an octopus. 🐙

Its skin holds millions of tiny bags of yellow, red or brown pigment. Muscles pull them open and nerves from the brain
run the muscles, so it can change in as little as a fifth of a second. Underneath, shiny cells bounce back blues and
greens, and white cells take on whatever light falls on them. Little bumps rise to copy the texture of rock.

The strange part: most scientists think octopuses are color-blind, and nobody knows for sure how they match. In one
study, octopus skin in a dish reacted to light on its own, but slowly, and it's unknown whether that helps.

## Noise cancelling (`noise-cancel-01`)
Noise-cancelling headphones don't just block sound. They make more of it. 🎧

Tiny mics listen to the hum around you, and a chip makes an upside-down copy. Where the noise pushes the air, the copy
pulls, and when push meets pull, most of the hum cancels right at your ear. Your music is left alone because the chip
knows what it's playing.

It works best on steady sounds like a plane engine. Quick, changing sounds like voices are harder, so the soft cushions
do some old-fashioned blocking too.

## Microwave (`microwave-01`)
Does your microwave cook food from the inside out? Nope. 🥔

The magnetron makes invisible waves that bounce around the metal box. The holes in the door are too small for them to
get out. The waves swing back and forth about 2.45 billion times a second, tugging on the water molecules in your
food. The molecules wiggle and bump into their neighbors, and that bumping is heat.

The waves only reach a short way in (about a centimeter in moist food), so the middle of thick food waits for the heat
to creep inward. Hot and cold spots are why the plate spins.

Simplified: microwaves also heat fats and sugars, and it isn't a "resonance" with water.

## Lightsaber (`lightsaber-01`)
How would a real lightsaber work? It can't be a laser. ⚔️

A laser beam never stops, and two lasers would pass straight through each other. So many fans picture plasma instead:
gas so hot it glows. Magnets in the hilt hold it in a thin tube, and in the old Legends books it loops back into the
hilt, which gives the blade its end. When two magnetic blades touch, their fields snap together and throw off a burst
of plasma. That's the clash.

The catch is power. No battery that small is anywhere near strong enough. Real plasma cutters run above 20,000 °C,
hotter than the Sun's surface (about 5,500 °C).

In canon, the blade is energy focused by a kyber crystal, and the plasma-and-magnets version is a fan and physics
thought experiment. The Death Star's superlaser runs on a hypermatter reactor, with giant kyber crystals channelling
the beam.

## Lactate (`lactate-run-01`)
Lactic acid isn't the trash your legs fill up with on a hard run. It's fuel. 🏃‍♂️🔥

When you run hard, sugar breaks down faster than your muscles' tiny engines can burn it, so the extra becomes lactate.
Your heart, your brain and other muscles grab it and burn it again. The burn in your legs comes from acid and other
leftovers together.

Run fast enough and lactate piles up faster than you can use it. That pace is your lactate threshold, and training
pushes it up, partly by growing more of those tiny engines.

Simplified for a one-minute video: the "tiny engines" are mitochondria, and fatigue has several causes, not one.
Next-day soreness isn't lactate (it clears within about an hour), and thresholds vary a lot between people.

## Cat landing (`cat-landing-01`)
Why do cats land on their feet? 🐈 It isn't the tail: cats with no tail do it too.

The inner ear and eyes find "up". With nothing to push on in mid-air, the cat bends in the middle so each half can turn
its own way. It tucks its front legs to spin the front, then swaps so the back catches up. Then it reaches down and
bends its legs to land softly, all in under half a second.

Cats can still get badly hurt in falls. Never drop a cat to test this, and keep window screens closed.

Simplified and slowed down: real cats mix "bend and twist" with "tuck and turn", and the tail helps a little.

## Black hole (`black-hole-01`)
What would happen if you fell into a black hole? 🕳️ Scientists really call it spaghettification.

Near a small, star-sized black hole, gravity pulls your feet much harder than your head, so you'd be stretched long and
thin before you even reach the edge. A friend watching from far away would see you slow down, stop near the edge, turn
red and fade away. Your own watch keeps ticking normally, and you fall right in.

Near a giant black hole, like the one at the center of our galaxy, you could cross the edge (the event horizon) in one
piece. Either way, once you cross, you can never come back out.

## AI image (`ai-image-01`)
AI image makers don't glue together bits of real photos. 🐕🏄🍕

Many of them start with pure static. Your words become numbers, and the AI guesses which part of the picture is noise
and wipes a little away, often 20 to 50 times. Each time, the whole picture gets clearer, with your words steering
every guess. It learned by practicing on billions of captioned pictures: covering each one in static, then cleaning it
back up.

Simplified: real models like Stable Diffusion clean a small hidden grid of numbers, then enlarge it into the full
picture. Not every image AI works this way; some newer ones, like GPT-4o's, build the picture piece by piece instead.
And very rarely, a model can reproduce a heavily duplicated training image almost exactly.
