# Lactate run 01: picture brainstorm (fresh agent, no production rules)

Picture ideas for lactate-run-01, grouped by line. I read only lines.txt. Every idea is something moving, never a static diagram with arrows, and each says what it can grow into so the next frame opens out of it. I suggest no black void: put everything in a warm, lit world (track at dusk, inside a glowing muscle, a kitchen) so it feels like a film rather than slides.

A suggested through-line object: one sugar cube (glucose) with a small face, called "Sugie". We follow it the whole way: it gets eaten by an engine, it gets split, it spills over, it gets caught and burned again. One focus point carries the viewer from frame to frame.

---

LINE 1: "Most people think lactic acid is waste that makes your legs burn."

1. Literal: a runner's thighs glow red from the inside, and orange flames lick up the shins in time with each stride. A label, "lactic acid = waste", stamps onto the flames like a sticker. The camera pushes into the glowing thigh to set up line 2.
2. Everyday analogy: a garbage truck backs into a leg ("beep beep beep") and dumps a pile of glowing yellow sludge into the muscle. The muscle fibres wince and start to smoke.
3. Wacky: the runner's legs are two chimneys pouring out black smoke, and a tiny factory inspector with a clipboard writes "POLLUTION". The smoke curls into the shape of the words "lactic acid".
4. Kid's drawing: a crayon stick figure with scribbled red zigzags on its legs and a thought bubble saying "ouchy acid". The crayon lines wobble as the figure runs.
5. Personified (sets up the twist): a grumpy green blob in a trash-bag cape, labelled "Lactic Acid", sits in the leg and pokes the muscle with a pitchfork. A crowd of tiny people boo it. Hold on the blob, because line 2 flips it.

LINE 2: "But actually, it's fuel."

1. Reversal, same object: the trash bag on the blob from line 1 rips open and inside is a glowing battery or fuel can. The pitchfork turns into a fuel nozzle. Same character, new meaning, no cut.
2. Everyday analogy: the garbage-truck sludge from idea 1.2 is poured into a car's tank, and the car roars off. Ding: "fuel".
3. Wacky: the red flames on the runner's legs from 1.1 are sucked backwards into a little lantern, which now lights the runner's path. The burn becomes the light.
4. Change of scale: a bin labelled "WASTE" zooms in until we see that the "trash" inside is stacked logs of firewood. A match strikes, and the fire warms the frame.
5. Kid's drawing: the crayon figure's red zigzags are crossed out, and a yellow lightning bolt is drawn over them.

LINE 3: "When you run, your muscles break down sugar to make energy."

1. Literal, moving: the camera dives through the skin into the muscle fibre (grown out of the fuel can from line 2). Sugar cubes (Sugie and friends) float in and get snapped in half by the fibres. Each break releases a spark that makes the fibre contract, and the runner's foot strikes the ground on the beat.
2. Everyday analogy: a runner's leg as a steam locomotive. A stoker shovels sugar cubes into the firebox, and every shovelful makes the wheels (feet) turn faster.
3. Kid's drawing: a big cookie gets chomped by a muscle with a mouth, and the muscle flexes its biceps with a "POW".
4. Personified: Sugie, a smiley sugar cube with a backpack, rides the bloodstream like a waterslide into the muscle and shouts "reporting for duty!". We follow Sugie into line 4.
5. Coin-machine analogy: sugar cubes go into a vending machine on the muscle, and out come gold coins stamped "ATP" that the leg spends on each stride. Coins clink at each footfall.

LINE 4: "At an easy pace, tiny engines in your muscles burn it all the way."

1. Literal: inside the fibre, bean-shaped mitochondria hum like little furnaces. Sugie's two halves slide into one and come out as gentle puffs of CO2 and water droplets, plus a stream of ATP coins. A calm, steady rhythm, like a jogger breathing.
2. Personified: the tiny engines are pudgy chefs at a slow-food grill. They turn each sugar piece carefully, cook it all the way through, and plate it with a satisfied chef's kiss. Nothing is left over and the plates come back empty.
3. Everyday analogy: a wood stove burns a log down to fine white ash. It is efficient and gives a cozy glow. A runner jogs past chatting easily, with a speech bubble saying "nice weather".
4. Change of scale: pull out from the one engine to show thousands of them glowing like city lights across the muscle at night, all steady and all even.
5. Kid's drawing: little smiling robots with chimneys munch sugar and puff out heart-shaped clouds.
Transition: the chefs' conveyor belt starts to speed up, which carries us into line 5.

LINE 5: "But when you run hard, sugar breaks down super fast. The tiny engines can't keep up."

1. Everyday analogy, best for the mechanism: the Lucy-and-Ethel chocolate factory. The chefs' conveyor belt from line 4 speeds up, sugar pieces pile up, the chefs stuff their cheeks and pockets, and the pieces fall off the end. The viewer sees the bottleneck instantly.
2. Literal, with motion: the runner shifts into a sprint, and the sugar-splitting in the fibre goes from a drip to a firehose of half-cubes. The mitochondria's intake doors jam with a queue like a nightclub line, and a bouncer holds up a "FULL" sign.
3. Wacky: a tiny engine in a hamster wheel spins so fast that its fur blows back and its cheeks flap, while sugar keeps raining on its head.
4. Funnel analogy: someone pours a whole bucket of water into a narrow funnel. The funnel (engine) takes what it can, and the rest bulges over the rim. Hold on the overflow, because it becomes line 6.
5. Change of scale: a single-lane toll booth with a horizon-long traffic jam of sugar cars honking behind it.

LINE 6: "So the extra turns into lactate, and it spills into your blood."

1. Continuation of the funnel: the overflow changes colour as it runs over the rim. Plain sugar water turns into glowing amber "lactate" droplets, which trickle out of the fibre wall into a red blood river that carries them off like lanterns floating downstream.
2. Personified: the leftover half-cubes in line put on a little amber jacket (now "Lactate") and squeeze out through a doggy door in the muscle wall, then cannonball into the blood stream, which is a red lazy river.
3. Everyday analogy: a kitchen with too many dinner orders. The overflow food gets packed into takeaway boxes, labelled, and handed out of the window to delivery cyclists (blood cells) who pedal it away. It isn't thrown out, it's sent elsewhere.
4. Wacky: the muscle sweats amber marbles that roll down a Rube Goldberg chute into the vein, which works like a marble run.
5. Kid's drawing: a bathtub overflowing with yellow bubbles that float out of the bathroom door and down a red road.
Transition: follow one amber lantern or box (Sugie in its new jacket) downstream into line 7.

LINE 7: "Your heart, your brain, and other muscles grab it and burn it again."

1. Personified relay: the amber Lactate box from line 6 travels through the blood river. A hand reaches out of the heart (pulsing like a drum) and grabs one, the brain catches one with a butterfly net, and a resting arm muscle snags one like a frog's tongue. Each one flares as it is burned, and the three glows light up the outline of the whole body.
2. Everyday analogy: a food-sharing route or leftovers night. One kitchen's extras are delivered to three neighbours who heat them up and eat. A sticker reads "nothing wasted".
3. Wacky: a body-wide pinball machine. The lactate ball pings off bumpers labelled HEART, BRAIN and ARM, and each hit lights the bumper up with points ("+energy").
4. Change of scale: pull back to a night-time satellite view of the body as a city map. The amber dots flow from the leg's "factory district" to the heart and brain "districts", which brighten as the lights come on.
5. Kid's drawing: a smiling heart and brain with forks and napkins, sitting at a table, eating glowing yellow nuggets.

LINE 8: "So why do your legs burn? Hard running also makes acid and other leftovers. Those help slow you down."

1. Reveal the real culprit, using the line 1 character: zoom back into the leg. Lactate, the hero, steps aside, and behind it are tiny spiky red sprites (acid, H+) and grey dust-bunny gremlins (other leftovers). They gum up the muscle's gears so the fibres squeak and move slower. The runner's stride visibly slows. The true villain was hiding behind the scapegoat.
2. Everyday analogy: a busy kitchen. The food (lactate) is fine, but the dirty dishes, smoke and grease (acid and leftovers) pile up until the chefs slow down and cough. The smoke alarm stings.
3. Wacky: lemon-juice raindrops fall on the muscle and every fibre puckers like it bit a lemon, scrunching up and sizzling. The runner's face puckers too.
4. Speed-governor framing (shows they are a brake, not just pain): little red hands push down on a brake pedal inside the knee, or sand is poured into a bike chain. The pace needle drops from sprint to jog.
5. Kid's drawing: spiky red "ouch bugs" with tiny forks, and a crayon arrow pointing at them labelled "the REAL burn".

LINE 9: "Run fast enough, and lactate piles up faster than you can use it. That pace is your lactate threshold."

1. Bathtub analogy, the most memorable: the blood is a bathtub. The tap (production) is turned up as the runner speeds up, and the drain (heart, brain and muscles using it) keeps pace, so the level stays flat. Then the tap passes the drain's speed and the water rises fast. A bright line painted on the tub wall where that happens is labelled "your threshold". The tap handle is literally the runner's speedometer.
2. Literal, grown from line 7: the deliveries to heart and brain from line 7 can't keep up. Amber boxes stack up in the blood river into a teetering tower that wobbles as the runner's pace dial ticks up. At one dial mark the stack suddenly shoots upward, and that mark glows: threshold.
3. Graph in a world, not on a slide: the runner is on a hill road that is the lactate curve itself. It is flat and easy, and then at one point it bends sharply upward into a cliff wall. A signpost on the bend reads "Threshold". The runner hits the bend and the camera tilts up the wall.
4. Wacky: a juggler (your body) juggling amber balls. More balls come faster, the juggler sweats, and at one exact tempo the balls start falling and bouncing everywhere. Freeze on that moment with the beat count shown: "this pace".
5. Change of scale: a dam. The reservoir rises slowly, then the spillway can't drain fast enough and water overtops the dam at a marked line.

LINE 10: "Training pushes that line, because you grow more tiny engines."

1. Literal, montage without cuts: inside the fibre, a calendar flips page after page and with each flip new mitochondria bud and multiply like popcorn popping. The threshold line from line 9 slides further up the tub wall, or the cliff bend from line 9 moves further down the road.
2. Personified: the chef crew from lines 4 and 5 recruit. New chefs arrive in tiny buses, new grills are installed, and the same fast conveyor belt from line 5 now gets handled smoothly. Nothing falls off.
3. Everyday analogy: a supermarket with one checkout and a long queue. With training, more checkout lanes open (ding, ding, ding) and the queue disappears.
4. Bathtub continuation (strongest if line 9 used the tub): with each week the drain gets wider, and drains are added. Now the tap can turn higher before the water rises, and the painted threshold line climbs up the wall.
5. Kid's drawing: the crayon figure from line 1 now has a little factory in each leg, and the drawing gets more chimneys each time a sticker star is added for a training day.

LINE 11: "So lactate isn't trash. It's backup fuel, and fit runners burn even more of it."

1. Bookend with line 1: the grumpy trash-blob is shown one last time. Its trash-bag cape blows away and it is revealed as a glowing amber superhero with a fuel-can emblem. The booing crowd from line 1 now cheers. It flies into a fit runner's leg and the runner's stride lifts.
2. Fit-runner contrast in one frame: two runners side by side. The beginner's amber lanterns float and pile up. The trained runner's legs and heart gobble the lanterns like Pac-Man, and they glow brighter and run easier.
3. Everyday analogy: a hybrid car. The petrol engine is the main sugar burn, and the battery recharged by braking is lactate used again. The dashboard shows a "regen" icon lighting up, labelled "backup fuel".
4. Change of scale, final pull-out: from the glowing engines in one fibre, out through the leg, to the runner, to a whole stadium of runners each glowing amber, and end on a city-wide glow.
5. Kid's drawing payoff: the very first crayon drawing (red zigzags, "ouchy acid") is crossed out, and the kid draws the legs as two batteries with a big smiling sun.

---

A few recurring things that will help it feel like a film and not a presentation:
- Never hold a static frame. Something always moves: the runner's stride rhythm can stay in the background as a heartbeat that speeds up in lines 5, 8 and 9 and relaxes in 4 and 10.
- Each frame's last moving object becomes the next frame's subject: the fuel can opens to the fibre, the conveyor belt overflows into the funnel, the overflow becomes a lantern in the blood, the lanterns pile up, the bathtub drain widens.
- Callbacks reward attention: the line 1 blob comes back in 11, the chefs from line 4 come back in 5 and 10, and the line 9 bathtub or cliff bend comes back in 10.
- Accuracy notes: the burn comes from acid (H+) and other leftovers such as phosphate, not from lactate itself (line 8). Lactate is shuttled out through the cell wall to the heart, brain and resting muscles (line 7). Training grows mitochondria and also improves clearance, so the line 10 "more drains" analogy is correct as well.
