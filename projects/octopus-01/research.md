# Research: How does an octopus turn invisible?

Collected 2026-11-07. **[O]** = I opened the page and read the fact there. **[S]** = I only saw it in search-result snippets (check it before using it on screen).

## 1. Chromatophores (colour sacs)
- Chromatophores are stretchy sacs of pigment in the top layer of the skin. Each one is ringed by radial muscles, "like the spokes of a bike" (Hanlon). When the muscles pull, the sac opens into a disc of colour. https://www.sciencefriday.com/articles/secrets-of-cephalopod-camouflage/ [O]
- The pigment colours are yellow, red and brown, in three layers (Hanlon; general cephalopod statement). [O, same Science Friday page]
- When the muscles relax, the sac shrinks back by itself because it is elastic. The muscles are run by motor neurons in the brain. https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/106509v2 [O] (Laurent lab, Max Planck; studied squid and cuttlefish)
- Hormones do not control them (Messenger 2001, *Biol. Rev.*). https://doi.org/10.1017/S1464793101005772 [S]
- Speed: cephalopods can change "color, brightness, contrast and pattern in as little as 200 milliseconds", about one eyeblink (Roger Hanlon, MBL, *Alert Diver* 2017). https://dan.org/alert-diver/article/natures-best-and-fastest-camouflage/ [O]
- Numbers: an octopus has "10 million to 20 million chromatophore organs" in its skin (Hanlon, Alert Diver [O]). Science Friday quotes him as "tens of millions" [O]. Neither source names a species, so say "an octopus" or "millions".
- Density: "some species of cuttlefish and octopus" have about 230 chromatophores per mm² as adults, which works out to about 23,000 per cm². https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/106509v2 [O] (the species are not named)

## 2. Iridophores and leucophores (the light reflectors)
- Order in the skin: chromatophores on top, iridophores in the middle, leucophores at the bottom. [O, Science Friday]
- Chromatophores are "generally located in a distinct layer above the iridophores." https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2706477 [O] (Mäthger, Denton, Marshall & Hanlon 2009, *J. R. Soc. Interface*)
- Iridophores "contain stacks of thin plates that reflect light by thin-film interference", like a soap bubble. Thinner plates reflect bluer light. This gives the shiny blues and greens, such as the blue rings of the blue-ringed octopus. [O, PMC2706477]
- Leucophores "reflect the ambient wavelengths of light": they look white in white light and red in red light, so they take on the colour of the light around them. Octopus and cuttlefish have them; most squid do not. [O, PMC2706477]
- The bottom layer "doesn't have any neurons; it always looks the same" (Hanlon). [O]

## 3. Papillae (skin bumps)
- Papillae are skin bumps that rise from flat to spiky to copy 3D texture. Only cephalopods can do this. An octopus has "perhaps several thousand" of them. [O, Alert Diver]
- They work as a muscular hydrostat (like your tongue): squeezing the ring-shaped muscles while relaxing the vertical ones makes "the bump go up" (Hanlon). [O, Science Friday]
- Locking: in the **European cuttlefish (*Sepia officinalis*)**, extended papillae stayed up for more than an hour without nerve signals. This points to a "catch-like" muscle mechanism like the one in clams. The nerve comes from the stellate ganglion, not the brain. Gonzalez-Bellido, Scaros, Hanlon & Wardill 2018, *iScience*. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/02/180215141803.htm [O]. The paper did not test octopuses.

## 4. Colour blindness: known vs open
- **Known:** shallow-water cephalopods have only one type of photoreceptor ("only one photoreceptor type exists"). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961147 [O] (Stubbs & Stubbs 2016, *PNAS*)
- **Known (cuttlefish):** behaviour tests show cuttlefish are "completely colorblind". They seem to match brightness and contrast: "cuttlefish camouflage themselves by matching intensities of objects in the environment." Mäthger et al. 2006, *Vision Research*. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/04/060419072340.htm [O]
- **Open:** Hanlon says "every bit of evidence we have says that they're color-blind", yet they match colours, and no one knows how. [O, Science Friday]
- **Hypothesis A, light-sensing skin.** In skin cut from the **California two-spot octopus (*Octopus bimaculoides*)**, chromatophores opened when light hit them, with no eye or brain attached (called "LACE"). The skin responded fastest to blue light. Opsin protein was found in sensory cells of the skin, not in the chromatophores. Ramirez & Oakley 2015, *J. Exp. Biol.* https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4448664/ [O]
  - The skin response is slow: a latency of about 6.5 s in adults (15 s in hatchlings), then about 5 s more to expand fully. Brain-driven changes take a fraction of a second. [O, PMC4448664]
  - Oakley: "We don't know yet how this is used, or indeed if it is used, in the living animal." https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/octopuses-and-maybe-squid-can-sense-light-with-their-skin [O]
- **Hypothesis B, odd pupils.** The off-centre pupils (U-shaped in squid and cuttlefish, a horizontal slit in shallow octopus) plus chromatic aberration might let one receptor type pick up colour from how blurry an edge looks. The model fails on flat single-colour fields. It is a computer model only and has not been tested on animals. A 2016 *PNAS* letter disputes it. [O PMC4961147; letter S]
- **Hypothesis C, brightness and pattern matching.** Supported in cuttlefish (Mäthger 2006). Leucophores also match ambient light passively.

## 5. Fun facts for kids
- Camouflage types: background matching (uniform), mottle, disruptive, masquerade (looking like an object), and mimicry (looking like another animal). [O, Alert Diver]
- **Mimic octopus (*Thaumoctopus mimicus*)**, Indo-Malayan region: it impersonates a lionfish (arms spread like spines), a banded sea snake (body buried, two arms showing) and a flatfish (Norman, Finn & Tregenza 2001, *Proc. R. Soc. B*). https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2001.1708 [S; the page returned 403]
- Sleeping colours: ***Octopus laqueus*** sleeps pale and still. About once an hour it has roughly a minute of "active sleep": its skin flashes through its awake patterns while its arms and eyes twitch. Pophale, Reiter et al. 2023, *Nature* (OIST). Reiter: "We currently don't know which of these explanations [incl. dreaming], if any, could be correct." https://phys.org/news/2023-06-octopus-similar-humans.html [O]
- Brain in the arms: ***O. vulgaris*** has about 500 million neurons, and "over two thirds… are located within their arms and body" (Gutnick et al. 2020, *Current Biology*). https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/11/201102120027.htm [O]

## 6. Model reference
- **Common octopus (*Octopus vulgaris*):** "distinctly warty" skin. Eight stout arms of about equal length, with the top (dorsal) pair a little shorter. Each arm has **two rows of suckers**. Colour is grey, yellow, brown or green. It lives on rocky coasts at 0–200 m. Total length is up to 1.3 m. https://www.marlin.ac.uk/species/detail/1117 [O]
  - Arms are about 3–4× the body length. [S, unnamed secondary source]
- **Day octopus (*Octopus cyanea*)**, a good choice for a reef scene: Indo-Pacific, from East Africa to Hawaii. It dens in coral reefs or digs holes in sand or coral rubble. Mantle is about 16 cm and arms about 80 cm (so arms are about 5× the mantle). It is usually brown to reddish brown with dark blue rings, and hunts by day. https://www.aquariumofpacific.org/onlinelearningcenter/species/day_octopus [O]
- Anatomy (general knowledge, not looked up): mantle behind the head, eyes raised on top, siphon on the side, arms joined by webbing around the mouth underneath.
- Reef palette (my suggestion): tan and grey rubble, green and brown algae, sand, with some coloured coral.

## Careful wording (avoid these)
- "Octopuses see with their skin." Too strong. Say: skin cut from one species reacts to light, mostly brightness. Whether this helps camouflage is unknown.
- "Octopuses can see colours." Unproven. The evidence points to colour blindness, and how they still match colours is an open question.
- "Each colour cell changes colour." No. Each sac has one pigment and opens or closes.
- "Hormones / mood change the colour." No. Nerves and muscles do it.
- "Invisible." Say "blends in".
- "Instant." Say "in a fraction of a second, as fast as 200 ms".
- "Papillae lock in octopuses." The locking was shown in cuttlefish.
- "Two-thirds of neurons are in the arms." The source says "arms and body"; say "most".
- "Octopuses dream." Say: their skin flashes colours during active sleep, and scientists don't know if they dream.
- "U-shaped pupils": octopus pupils are a slit, and the idea is untested.
- Don't attach the 10–20 million total to *O. vulgaris*; no species is given.
