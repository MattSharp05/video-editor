# AI image 01: picture brainstorm (fresh agent, no production rules)

Picture ideas for projects/ai-image-01 (10 lines). I read only lines.txt.

These ideas follow the creator's rules: no black void (backgrounds are warm, coloured, or a real place); no cuts; every frame grows out of one point of focus in the frame before; something is always changing (no frames where nothing moves but arrows).

SUGGESTED SPINE (one continuous move for the whole film)
The phone is the "camera". We start on the phone, push into its screen, and the picture stays inside that screen for the whole middle. At the end we pull back out and the phone is in a hand again. Each transition grows from one carried object:
- painter's dot → static grain → the prompt text box → the send arrow → number tiles → TV static → the image → a training photo → back to the phone.
For each line, the marked idea is the one I'd pick ("best bet"), plus a note on the "grow" transition into the next line.

---

LINE 1: "Most people think AI paints your picture one dot at a time."
1. Literal (myth shown, then broken): a tiny robot arm with a fine brush dabs pixels one by one onto a phone-sized canvas, left to right like a printer. A progress bar reads "0.0001%". The arm yawns.
2. Everyday analogy: a dot-to-dot / colour-by-number sheet. A pencil inches from dot 1 to dot 2 … to dot 3,000,000. The counter keeps rising and the page never fills.
3. Wacky: a tiny painter in a beret sits on the phone and paints one dot at a time with a single bristle. A shrinking hourglass and a cobweb grow on his easel. (BEST BET: funny, and it sets up the myth to be broken.)
4. Kid's drawing: a crayon robot holding a giant pencil, drawing a dog one dot at a time, with a speech bubble "this will take forever."
5. Scale: start on one dot huge in frame. Pull back and it's one of millions on a grid, with "1 of 2,000,000" ticking.
Grow: the painter taps his last dot, and it fizzes and blooms into static that floods the canvas (into line 2).

LINE 2: "But actually, most AI pictures start as fuzzy static."
1. Literal: the dot fizzes outward into a full screen of coloured snow. The painter falls off his stool.
2. Analogy: a snow globe that's just been shaken. Pure flurry, no scene yet. Hold it, and a hint of a shape is waiting inside.
3. Wacky / personified: the static is a crowd of tiny jittery pixel-creatures all shouting at once. A "SHHH" sign appears and a few of them freeze in place.
4. Analogy: a block of marble with a sculptor's chisel resting on it ("the statue is already in there"). Here the static is the marble. (BEST BET for remembering it: it says the image is uncovered, not painted. Could be a quick beat over the snow.)
5. Kid's drawing: a scribbled-over page, "it starts as a mess!"
Grow: the static settles into the grey frosted background of a phone's text box. A cursor blinks in it (into line 3).

LINE 3: "You type: a dog surfing on a pizza. Then you hit send." (the creator asked for this exactly)
1. Literal (required): thumbs type on the phone, with key pops and the text appearing letter by letter, including a typo fixed with backspace for life. The send button pulses, the thumb hits it, and a satisfying squash.
2. Wacky: each word as it's typed hops out of the keyboard as a little character: "dog" wags, "surfing" wobbles on a board, "pizza" is a slice with legs. They queue up in the text box.
3. Personified send: the send arrow is a paper plane. On tap it folds itself and launches, and the camera chases it (BEST BET: it's the camera's guide into the next frame).
4. Scale: zoom with the message bubble as it leaves the phone, down a glowing cable, into a server warehouse whose doors open for it.
5. Kid's version: a hand-drawn phone with a big red "SEND" button and "WHOOSH!" lines.
Grow: the paper plane/bubble flies into a machine slot. Camera follows it inside, where the words hit a sorting wall (into line 4).

LINE 4: "Computers can't read words, so the AI turns them into numbers."
1. Literal: the words slide into a slot and come out the other side as columns of numbers (0.82, -1.3, 0.07 …) that click into place like an odometer.
2. Everyday analogy: a vending machine / coin sorter. Words drop in and number coins clatter out into labelled tubes. Or a barcode scanner beeps over the word "dog" and a barcode of numbers prints.
3. Wacky / personified: a confused robot holds the word "dog" upside down ("??"). A translator robot hands it a sheet of numbers instead and its eyes light up "ahh."
4. Map version (mechanism-true: embeddings are places in a meaning space): each word becomes a pin dropped on a map. "Dog" lands near "puppy" and "wolf", "pizza" lands near "pasta", and its coordinates are the numbers. (BEST BET for understanding: it shows numbers that carry meaning, not random codes.)
5. Kid's version: a word goes into a meat grinder and numbers come out like spaghetti.
Grow: the number tiles spin like slot-machine reels, blur into grey noise, and the noise becomes the TV screen (into line 5). Or: the numbers drip down onto a canvas as the "recipe" card, clipped to the side for line 8.

LINE 5: "Then it makes a picture of pure static, like an old TV."
1. Literal: an old CRT TV with rabbit-ear antennas hisses with snow. Its screen is exactly the phone's aspect ratio.
2. Wacky: a chef sprinkles static from a shaker onto a blank plate/canvas like salt, "pure noise, seasoned to taste."
3. Personified: the TV is a grumpy character, antennas twitching, static as its "bad hair day."
4. Scale: zoom into the static. Each grain is a random-coloured pixel tile flipping colour every frame, like a slot machine of millions.
5. Analogy: a fogged-up bathroom mirror, all mist.
Best bet: the CRT TV, with the camera pushing into it until its screen fills the frame. Keep it there for lines 6–8 so the image clears in one place (no cuts). Seen from inside, the TV frame becomes the phone frame.

LINE 6: "It guesses which part is just noise, and wipes a little away."
1. Literal: a soft squeegee/eraser passes over the static. A thin layer peels off and faint blotches of colour show underneath.
2. Everyday analogy: wiping a fogged mirror with your hand. One swipe, and a blurry shape is behind it. Or cleaning a muddy dog with one sponge pass.
3. Wacky / personified: a tiny detective with a magnifying glass inspects the static, tags some grains "NOISE," and a vacuum sucks just those up. It's careful: only a little.
4. Scratch-card: a coin scratches a lottery card a tiny bit at a time.
5. Kid's version: a hand with a big eraser, "rub rub."
Best bet: the mirror wipe. Everyone knows it, and the image behind is "already there". Show the guess as a ghost outline of the noise lifting off, then fading. Make it clear it removes noise, not adds paint.
Grow: the wipe leaves a faint dog-shaped smudge. The camera locks onto that smudge (into line 7).

LINE 7: "It does that about twenty to fifty times. Each time, the whole picture gets clearer."
1. Literal: a step counter "1… 2… 3… 50". With each tick the whole frame sharpens a notch, so you see the global refinement and not a top-to-bottom wipe. Stress "the whole picture" to contrast with the line 1 myth.
2. Everyday analogy: focusing a camera lens / binoculars, click by click, as a person turns the ring. Or a Polaroid developing, sped up.
3. Wacky / montage: a flipbook. A thumb flicks the pages and each page is a little clearer. The pages fan out and the image lands.
4. Scale / pace: a speedometer or a "spin cycle" washing-machine drum. The image spins in the porthole, each spin cleaner, and comes out fresh.
5. Personified: the pixel-crowd from line 2, after each "SHHH", jogs to its correct place. By step 50 they're standing in formation as the image.
Best bet: the Polaroid/lens focus, with a tick counter in the corner. It's continuous change in a fixed frame, so it avoids the boring moon-and-tide problem: the picture itself transforms in every frame.

LINE 8: "Your words steer every guess. So the blobs become a dog, a wave, and a pizza."
1. Literal: the prompt words from line 3 float above the canvas like magnets. Blobs in the static drift toward them and snap into a dog, a wave, a pizza.
2. Everyday analogy: cloud-watching. Someone points "that's a dog!" and the cloud obeys and becomes one. Or a GPS voice: "turn left for dog."
3. Wacky / personified: the three words wear coach whistles and yell at their blobs. "Dog" barks orders, the blob grows ears. "Pizza" flings pepperoni. The surf wave curls on cue.
4. Fork-in-the-road: the same static shown twice, side by side, steered by different words ("dog surfing pizza" vs "cat on a cake"). It ends up as two different pictures. (BEST BET for understanding steering: the same start with different words gives a different result. Keep it in one frame by splitting the canvas, then let the cat side slide away.)
5. Kid's version: magnets pulling iron filings into a dog shape.
Payoff: the final sharp image, a dog on a pizza surfboard on a wave. Let it move (spray, dog's ears flapping), like a "movie" not a slide.
Grow: the image shrinks into one photo on a huge wall of photos with captions (into line 9).

LINE 9: "How did it learn? It practiced on billions of pictures with captions. It covered each one in static, then learned to clean it up."
1. Literal: a giant scrolling wall of captioned photos ("cat on sofa", "red car", "beach at sunset"). One is pulled out, buried in static a little at a time, and the AI wipes it back, with a gold star when it matches.
2. Everyday analogy: a "spot the original" practice drill, or a kid learning by scrubbing mud off toys whose names are written on them. Or a sculptor practising by covering statues in sand and brushing it off.
3. Wacky / gym: training montage music, a robot doing "reps". The photo is dunked in static, then cleaned, again and again, with a sweatband and a counter "1… 1,000,000… 1,000,000,000".
4. Scale: pull back from one practice photo to a library of billions, a city of shelves, to show the size.
5. Rewind trick (mechanism-true): play a photo going into static forward, then play it backwards. "It learned to run this backwards." (BEST BET: one picture explains training and generation together.)
6. Kid's version: a teacher holding flashcards, a messy one and a clean one, the robot raising its hand.
Grow: the last practice photo turns, becomes a phone screen, and the phone is in a hand (into line 10).

LINE 10: "So words become numbers, static gets cleaned, and a brand new picture lands on your phone."
1. Literal recap in one continuous move: the camera flies backward through the whole film. The words fall into number tiles, the tiles into static, the static wipes clean, and the image drops onto the phone with a notification "ding."
2. Everyday analogy: a recipe/pipeline. A three-step conveyor belt (words, then numbers, then static, then clean picture) ends in a delivery box landing on the phone: "your order has arrived."
3. Wacky: the dog surfs out of the static, off the pizza, and right onto the phone screen. A wave splashes the user's thumb.
4. Personified: the paper plane from line 3 returns carrying the finished picture and lands on the phone.
5. Kid's version: arrows linking a crayon chain of "ABC → 123 → fuzz → dog!"
Best bet: the paper plane returning. It closes the loop on the creator's required phone moment. The ending "lands on your phone" is literal, and it's the same object that opened the journey.

---

NOTES
- Accuracy: line 1 says "most", which is right. Some newer image models do build pictures piece by piece, so don't make the line 1 myth a flat "never."
- Line 4: the map-of-meaning picture is truer than random digits.
- Line 6: show it taking noise away. Showing it painting adds the wrong idea.
- Moon/tide lesson: the busiest risk is lines 5–7, where the frame is fixed. Keep the image itself transforming in every frame, and have the camera slowly drift or push in. Don't rely on labels popping on and off.
