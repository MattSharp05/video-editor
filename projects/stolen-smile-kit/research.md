# The Stolen Smile (Mona Lisa theft, 1911): research (fresh research agent, 2026-11-07)

## A. Sanity check and recommendation

| Case | Primary sources | No living private victims | No gore | 3 acts with an ending | Visuals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mona Lisa, 1911** | Strong: police files, trial, 1911–14 newspapers, Louvre material | Yes. The victim is a museum, and everyone involved has died | Yes | Yes. Theft, two-year hunt, Florence sting, painting goes home | Very strong |
| Gardner, 1990 | Good (FBI) | The guards who were tied up may still be alive | Yes | **No.** Unsolved, nothing recovered | Good (empty frames) |
| Antwerp diamonds, 2003 | Mostly the thief's own disputed story (Wired, 2009) | The victims are private diamond dealers, some probably alive | Yes | Partial. Convictions, but the diamonds were never found | Good |
| D.B. Cooper, 1971 | FBI files | The flight crew were threatened | A bomb threat on a plane is too dark for kids | **No.** Unsolved | Medium |

**Recommendation:** Go with the Mona Lisa. It is the only one of the four that is solved, has a clear ending (the painting goes home), hurt nobody, and has no living private victims. It also has a built-in twist: the theft is what made the painting famous. Gardner and Cooper have no ending. Antwerp depends on one man's story that others dispute, and its victims are private businesses.

## B1. Timeline

- **1908–1911:** Peruggia (born 8 Oct 1881 in Dumenza, Italy) works at the Louvre for the glazing firm Gobier. He cuts and cleans glass for the protective cases, including the Mona Lisa's. Police records call him a painter-decorator, not a carpenter.
- **Sunday 20 Aug 1911, how he got in. Sources disagree:**
  - **Hid overnight in a storeroom.** Supported by Hoobler, Smithsonian (drawing on Scotti), History.com and the Louvre's own kids' video. Scotti, the Smithsonian and the Louvre video say two helpers were with him, the Lancelotti brothers.
  - **Walked in about 7 a.m. Monday in his white work smock, alone.** This is Peruggia's own account in his interrogation and at trial (Wikipedia; monalisamissing.com).
  - Hoobler notes that police found signs someone had spent the night in a storage closet.
- **Monday 21 Aug, about 7:00–7:30 a.m.** The museum is closed (it closed every Monday). He lifts the painting off its **four hooks** in the Salon Carré. At 7:20 a.m. a maintenance chief, Picquet, walks through and notices nothing unusual. At 8:35 a.m. Picquet sees the painting gone but assumes the photographers have it (Hoobler).
- **Service stairwell:** he strips off the frame and the glass box and leaves them there.
- **The locked door:** the door at the bottom of the stairs is locked and his key doesn't work. He removes the doorknob with a screwdriver. **Sauvet, a Louvre plumber**, takes him for a fellow worker and opens the door with his own key and pliers (Hoobler; The American Magazine). Peruggia admitted this was the door to the **Cour du Sphinx**. Hoobler's route continues through the Cour du Sphinx, a gallery and the **Cour Visconti** to the main entrance, whose guard had gone to fetch water. A passer-by saw a man throw a metal object into a ditch; it was later identified as the doorknob.
- **Tuesday 22 Aug:** the painter **Louis Béroud** comes to paint the Mona Lisa and finds four bare hooks. Guards first assume it is with the photographers (Brigadier Poupardin; the Braun studio). The empty frame is found in the stairwell, and the news breaks that evening. The theft had gone unnoticed for about 26–28 hours.
- **The museum closed for a week.** When it reopened, thousands came to stare at the empty wall, Franz Kafka among them (Smithsonian).
- **The reward:** newspapers did offer rewards (Paris-Journal paid Pieret for his story). **I could not confirm an amount, so don't state one.**
- **7 Sept 1911:** the poet Apollinaire is arrested and held at La Santé prison. Back in 1907, his former secretary **Géry Pieret** had taken Iberian statuettes from the Louvre and sold two to Picasso. After the theft, Pieret bragged to Paris-Journal. **Picasso was questioned**, and both men were cleared. Apollinaire's time in jail is given as 3 days (Louvre video), 6 days, or about a week (Wikipedia). Safe wording: "a few days."
- **Police miss him:**
  - Police questioned Peruggia twice. A detective (Brunet, in Hoobler) searched his room and found nothing.
  - There was a thumbprint on the glass or frame, and his prints were on file from an earlier arrest. Hoobler says the files were sorted by body measurements (Bertillon's system), so the print couldn't be looked up. Wikipedia says police simply didn't check.
  - The popular "left thumb vs right thumb" story appears only in secondary sources (Mail & Guardian and others). **It is not well established; treat it as weak.**
- **About 28 months in Paris:** he keeps it in his room at **5 rue de l'Hôpital-Saint-Louis**, near the Gare de l'Est. Where exactly is disputed:
  - Smithsonian and History.com: a trunk with a false bottom.
  - His daughter (via monalisamissing.com): first on a table under a cloth, later in a crate with a false bottom.
  - Edsel: hanging over his kitchen table.
- **Nov 1913:** he writes to Florence art dealer **Alfredo Geri**, giving a Paris post-office box. The date is 24 Nov (Finestre sull'Arte) or received 29 Nov (The American Magazine). The signature is given as "Leonardo," "Leonard" or "Leonardo V." He asks 500,000 lire.
- **10 Dec 1913:** Peruggia, thin, in a suit and tie with a handsome moustache, visits Geri's shop. **11 Dec:** Geri and **Giovanni Poggi**, director of the Uffizi, come to the **Hotel Tripoli-Italia** on Via de' Panzani (room 20, third floor; later renamed Hotel La Gioconda). He pulls the painting, wrapped in red silk, from a white trunk with a false bottom. At the Uffizi, Poggi matches its seal and pattern of fine cracks. Peruggia is arrested at the hotel on 11 Dec (History.com) or 12 Dec (others).
- **Dec 1913, shown in Italy:** the Uffizi in Florence; in Rome, the French Embassy (Palazzo Farnese) and the Borghese Gallery over Christmas week; then **Milan's Brera, 29–30 Dec**, with over 60,000 visitors in a day and a half (Il Giorno, search snippet only).
- **31 Dec 1913:** arrives in Paris. **4 Jan 1914:** back in its place in the Salon Carré (Louvre video; Hoobler).
- **Trial:** opens **4 June 1914** in Florence. Sentence: **1 year 15 days**, cut on appeal to about **7 months**. He had already served about 7 months 9 days, so he walked free.
  - Wrong elsewhere: Wikipedia's Mona Lisa article says 6 months, Smithsonian says 8, Finestre says 1.5 years.
  - Later life: served in the Italian army in WWI, then lived in France. Died 8 Oct 1925, on his 44th birthday.
- **Motive:** he said patriotism, because Italian art had been taken "especially in the Napoleonic era." **That is wrong:** Leonardo brought the painting to France himself, and King Francis I acquired it. His letters to his father point to money ("Paris is where I will make my fortune," 22 Dec 1911).
- **Valfierno:** the story of a forger-mastermind comes only from **Karl Decker, Saturday Evening Post, 1932**. There is no supporting evidence, so call it a later, doubtful tale.

## B2. How the theft made it the world's most famous painting

- Before 1911, critics praised it from the 1860s on, but mostly among art lovers. Baedeker's Paris guidebook gave it one paragraph in 1878 and two sentences in 1907 (Smithsonian). One art historian: it "wasn't even the most famous painting in its gallery."
- After the theft it was front-page news worldwide (Reuters, 23 Aug 1911). The NYT wrote that citizens had "turned amateur Sherlock Holmeses" (quoted via History.com). There were satirical postcards, a short film and cabaret songs (Smithsonian), and crowds came to see an empty wall.
- At least 120,000 people saw it in the first two days after it came back (History.com). Dianne Hales: "She returned as public property, the first mass art icon." Today about 8 million people a year see it. Donald Sassoon's *Becoming Mona Lisa* makes the same case.

## B3. Details for the visuals

- **Painting:** 77 × 53 cm (about 30 × 21 in), oil on a poplar panel, about 18 lb (about 8 kg). With frame, brace and glass box, nearly 200 lb (Hoobler). Don't use Hoobler's "three slabs, 1.5 in thick"; it doesn't fit the Louvre's description.
- **Salon Carré:** a big square room hung floor to ceiling with Old Masters: Leonardo, Titian, Raphael, Correggio, Veronese, Rembrandt, Velázquez (Hoobler). It hung between **Correggio's *Mystic Marriage of St Catherine*** and **Titian's *Allegory of Alfonso d'Avalos***, with Veronese's huge *Wedding at Cana* nearby. That placement is from search snippets citing Scotti; I couldn't open a source, so treat it as likely but not verified.
- **Peruggia:** about 160 cm (5'3"), slim, moustache, white Louvre work smock.
- **Places:** the service stairwell, the door with its knob removed, the Cour du Sphinx and Cour Visconti, the room at 5 rue de l'Hôpital-Saint-Louis, the white trunk with a false bottom, the red silk wrapping, the hotel in Florence.

## B4. Where sources disagree, and safe wordings

- "He hid under his smock" → "He wrapped it in his smock" or "slipped out with it." He was small, so carrying it under the smock is doubtful; his own account is that he wrapped it and carried it under his arm.
- "He hid overnight in a closet" → "Some say he hid overnight; he said he walked in that morning."
- "He acted alone" or "he had two helpers" → "Police never fully settled whether he had help."
- "His left thumbprint fooled the police" → "He left a thumbprint, but the police files couldn't match it."
- "Napoleon stole it" → "He believed it had been taken from Italy. Actually, Leonardo brought it to France himself."
- "It was always the most famous painting" → "Before 1911, mostly art lovers knew it."
- "Picasso was arrested" → "Picasso was questioned." Only Apollinaire was jailed.
- "Hidden under his bed for two years" → "Hidden in his little room for over two years."
- Arrest date: "In December 1913."
- Sentence: "About seven months in jail."
- Discovery: say "the next day." It was 21 Aug, but some sources date the theft 22 Aug because that is when it was found.

## B5. Cliffhangers and hooks

**Ending for part 1:**
1. "Tuesday morning, a painter walks in to copy her… and finds only four empty hooks."
2. "Police find the empty frame on the stairs. The thief is gone, and so is she."
3. "The police knock on the thief's door. They look around his room… and leave."

**Ending for part 2:**
1. "Two years later, an art dealer in Florence opens a letter signed 'Leonardo.'"
2. "In a small hotel room, a man lifts the false bottom of a trunk…"
3. "The museum expert has to answer one question: is this the real one?"

**Hooks, each true as worded:**
1. "Most people think the Mona Lisa was always the world's most famous painting. Actually, getting stolen made her a superstar."
2. "More people came to see her empty wall than had come to see the painting itself." Use this wording carefully; the softer "Crowds lined up to stare at an empty wall" is safest.
3. "The thief had helped make the glass box that protected her."
4. "A plumber held the door open for the thief." Bonus.
