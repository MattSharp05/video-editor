# The Stolen Smile: picture brainstorm (fresh agent, no production rules)

Sources: the three `lines.txt` scripts and `stolen-smile-kit/research.md`. Nothing else was read.
Each idea is something we see and what happens to it. The kinds are literal, analogy, wacky,
scale, personified and kid-drawn. Each line ends with **Grows into next**: the single point of
focus that carries the eye into the next line without a cut. A **Pick** marks the idea I would
try first.

---

## 1. Series-wide devices

### 1.1 The four hooks (recurring object, and the way each part opens and ends)
Four black iron hooks in a row, like four dots. They are the series' logo and its clock.
- **Part 1** opens with the hooks holding her and ends with them **empty** ("only four empty hooks").
- **Part 2** opens on the empty hooks. The four of them bend into the **"?"** of "Who did it?", and
  at the reopening crowds stare at them. Part 2 ends with the four hooks still empty, while far
  away a letter is opened.
- **Part 3** ends with her being **hung back** on the same four hooks, and each one gives a little
  "clink" as it takes the weight.
- Running visual: whenever time passes, the four hooks quietly gather rust, dust or a cobweb.

### 1.2 The cloth that travels (transition device)
A piece of cloth is always wrapped around her, and pulling it is how frames open:
the **white smock** (part 1, she is wrapped and carried out), the **tablecloth / blanket in the
little room** (part 2), the **red silk** (part 3, when it is pulled away she is revealed). Each
part's title opens as the cloth is pulled off the frame like a magician's sheet. Where it falls,
the next place is revealed underneath. This gives a "no cuts" grammar: the cloth wipes, wraps and
unfolds every scene change, and it always has a reason in the story.

### 1.3 She is the only witness (personified running gag)
The painting is alive in a small way: only her **eyes** and the corners of her **smile** move.
She sees everything and can't say a word.
- When nobody stops him, her eyes slide sideways towards Vincenzo, as if saying "...anyone?"
- When the police search his room, she is right there under the cloth, and her eyes peek over
  the edge as they leave.
- Each part has one "smile meter" moment: her smile gets slightly bigger every time she becomes
  more famous. The last frame of part 3 shows her biggest (still subtle) smile.
- **Never** a cartoon mouth that talks. It works because it stays small: she remains a painting.

### 1.4 The fame counter (a number that ties the hook to the ending)
A small brass **turnstile / tally counter** of "people who know her". Part 1, line 2: it reads a
handful, and those people are a few art lovers with monocles. It ticks up with every newspaper,
song and postcard in part 2, jumps at the 100,000 in part 3, and in the final line spins into
"millions every year". It's the payoff of the hook ("getting stolen made her famous") turned into
one object that changes.

### 1.5 The smile as a path (optional single point of focus)
The curve of her smile is reused as a line the camera can travel along: the curve of a staircase
rail, a street, the arc of a train track to Italy, the curl of the "L" in "Leonardo". Every part
starts zoomed into the smile and ends pulling back to it, so the whole series is one journey
along one curve.

### 1.6 Parts open and end (summary)
| | Opens on | Ends on (cliffhanger) | The thing carried into the next part |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Her smile, very close, then the fame counter on "a handful" | Four empty hooks; her eyes looking at us from darkness under a smock | The empty hooks |
| 2 | Empty hooks, which become "?" | The letter's signature "Leonardo" in wet ink | The ink signature |
| 3 | The signature's ink drop, which spreads into the map of Florence | Her on the four hooks, the fame counter spinning, and her smile | (series end: zooms back into the smile where part 1 began) |

## 2. Three possible overall looks (each one avoids the black "presentation" background)

### Look A: "The Extra Edition" (1911 newspaper and silent film)
The whole world is printed on **cream newsprint**: halftone dots, ink, headline type, and
silent-film intertitle frames with ornate borders that are physical objects in the scene (they
can be picked up, torn or folded). The camera moves through columns of newsprint, and a picture in
one article becomes the next scene when we push into it. Colour: sepia and ink, with exactly
**one** colour, the warm ochre of the painting itself, so she glows on every page.
*Strength:* newspapers are part of the story (part 2 is literally newspapers making her famous).
*Risk:* lots of text on screen; keep headlines to 2–4 words.

### Look B: "The Toy Theatre" (paper diorama / pop-up book)
Everything is cut paper standing in layers on a little stage: the Louvre as a pop-up book, the
hall as paper arches with depth, Vincenzo as a flat paper figure on a stick, the police with
moustaches like paper cut-outs. Transitions **unfold**: a wall folds down to become a floor, a
door opens and a whole new street pops up behind it. Lit warmly, with real paper shadows.
*Strength:* every "new frame grows from the old one" by folding, and it's fun and handmade.
*Risk:* paper figures feel static unless they wobble, bounce and get pushed around.

### Look C: "Night Gouache Storybook" (warm painted storybook with a noir accent)
Thick painted brushstrokes, rich blues for the night museum and warm lamplight yellow for
interiors; long shadows from a single lamp like a gentle noir. Faces are simple and expressive.
The Mona Lisa is the only thing painted in the actual Leonardo style, so she always looks
"real" next to the cartoon world.
*Strength:* the strongest emotions (the empty wall, the reveal in red silk).
*Risk:* the most expensive to make; needs a consistent painting style in code (textures,
brush masks).

*My suggestion:* Look B as the base, with newspaper pages (Look A) as physical props inside the
diorama in part 2. Paper is cheap to animate in code and folds naturally.

## 3. Pictures for every line

### Part 1: the vanishing

**1.1 "Lots of people think the Mona Lisa was always the most famous painting in the world."**
- *Literal:* Close on her smile. We pull back and she's hanging today in front of a sea of raised
  phones, all flashes going off.
- *Analogy:* She's on a giant podium with a gold medal reading "No. 1", among other paintings
  holding silver and bronze.
- *Wacky:* Her portrait is printed on everything around her: mugs, socks, a pizza, a cat's
  sweater; the camera keeps pulling back and finding more.
- *Personified:* She smiles smugly, as if she's always been the star.
- *Kid-drawn:* A crayon drawing of a lady with a crown and "#1 FAMOUS" written above her.
- **Pick:** the sea of phones. On "think", a big red **"?"** stamp slams onto the podium/phones.
- **Grows into next:** the flashes freeze and become sepia; the phones melt back into a 1911
  frame.

**1.2 "But actually, before 1911, mostly art lovers knew her. Then someone stole her."**
- *Literal:* The same wall in 1911: she hangs among many paintings in the crowded Salon Carré,
  and three gentlemen with monocles admire a different, bigger painting.
- *Scale:* The fame counter is introduced: a brass tally counter that reads "a handful". One art
  lover leans in, the counter clicks to the next number.
- *Personified:* She looks to her left and right at the bigger paintings, a bit left out.
- *Wacky:* On "Then someone stole her", a hand reaches in from the edge of the frame and she's
  **gone**, with a "zip"; the monocle of an art lover pops out in shock.
- *Kid-drawn:* A stick figure running away with a rectangle, with speed lines.
- **Pick:** the left-out painting plus the hand that zips her away and the popping monocle.
- **Grows into next:** the empty space where she hung becomes the frame for the title.

**1.3 "This is The Stolen Smile. Part one: the vanishing."**
- *Literal:* The title is written in the empty frame.
- *Wacky:* Just her **smile** stays behind floating in the empty frame like the Cheshire Cat,
  then also slips away; the title appears where it was.
- *Analogy:* A magician's sheet (the smock, device 1.2) is pulled off an empty frame: "ta-da",
  nothing is there.
- *Kid-drawn:* "The Stolen Smile" in chunky letters, with the "o" of Stolen replaced by an empty
  frame.
- **Pick:** the Cheshire-cat smile floating, then vanishing; "Part one: the vanishing" appears
  as the last bit of it fades.
- **Grows into next:** the smile curve stays as a line and becomes the curve of the Seine around
  the Louvre.

**1.4 "Paris, 1911. The Louvre is a giant museum, and on Mondays it's closed."**
- *Literal:* A morning view of Paris and the long Louvre beside the river.
- *Scale:* The Louvre is so long that the camera travels along it and it never ends; a tiny
  pigeon flies along it and gets tired.
- *Analogy:* A giant **"Closed"** sign is hung on it like on a shop door, with "Monday" on it.
- *Personified:* The Louvre "sleeps" on Mondays: its windows are closed eyelids, and it snores
  a little; a nightcap is on the dome.
- *Kid-drawn:* A very long house with 100 windows and the word "SHUT".
- **Pick:** the sleeping Louvre with eyelid shutters. Perfectly explains why nobody is around.
- **Grows into next:** we go in through one window (one "eyelid" lifts) into the quiet hall.

**1.5 "One Monday morning, a small man in a white work smock walks through the quiet halls."**
- *Literal:* A small figure in a white smock walking down a long gallery, footsteps echoing.
- *Scale:* The hall is huge and he is tiny; paintings tower over him like buildings.
- *Analogy:* He's like a white chess pawn sliding across a giant empty chessboard floor.
- *Personified:* The portraits on the walls turn their eyes to follow him, one by one.
- *Kid-drawn:* A little white ghost-shape with legs walking past big rectangles.
- **Pick:** portraits whose eyes follow him one after another, a wave of eyes; it's the only
  movement in a quiet hall and gives life without dialogue.
- **Grows into next:** we push in on his face as he passes the last portrait.

**1.6 "His name is Vincenzo. He knows this place. He helped make the glass box that protects the Mona Lisa."**
- *Literal:* Close on Vincenzo (slim, moustache). A flashback through glass: him fitting the
  glass case around her, cleaning it.
- *Analogy:* Like a locksmith who builds a safe and keeps the key: he holds the glass box like
  a key.
- *Wacky:* His name is written on the glass with his breath (fogging glass), then wiped clean by
  his sleeve.
- *Personified:* Inside the glass box, she looks at him through the glass and recognises him
  ("oh, the glass man").
- *Kid-drawn:* A man holding a square of glass with a big smile, labelled "VINCENZO, HE MADE THE
  BOX".
- **Pick:** name written in his breath on the glass, then the wipe reveals her behind it: a
  reflection of his face over hers. The two main characters are one image.
- **Grows into next:** the reflection fades and we're in her room facing her.

**1.7 "He walks into her room. She hangs on the wall on four iron hooks."**
- *Literal:* The Salon Carré, crowded with big paintings; she's small among them, hung on four
  iron hooks.
- *Scale:* Push in to the hooks until they are like huge black steel anchors; one hook is as big
  as Vincenzo.
- *Analogy:* Like a coat on four coat-hooks (a hanger), or a picture in a kid's room.
- *Personified:* The four hooks are like four little fingers holding her up.
- *Kid-drawn:* A frame with four dots above it, numbered 1–4.
- **Pick:** the 1–2–3–4 count of the hooks, each one with a "clink" sound and a highlight. This
  plants the series' logo (device 1.1).
- **Grows into next:** his two hands come into frame on her edges.

**1.8 "He lifts her off. Nobody stops him."**
- *Literal:* He lifts the painting off; the hooks are left behind.
- *Wacky:* A long pause. All the other paintings in the room look on in silence. A cricket
  chirps.
- *Personified:* Her eyes slide to the side toward the door: "anyone?" (device 1.3).
- *Analogy:* Like taking the last cookie from the jar while everyone is asleep.
- *Kid-drawn:* A man lifting a box, with a speech bubble from the painting: "Help?"
- **Pick:** the silent other paintings and the cricket, plus her sideways eyes. This is the comedy
  beat of part 1.
- **Grows into next:** we follow her, held under his arm, through a small service door.

**1.9 "He carries her to a back staircase. There, he takes off her heavy frame and glass, and leaves them behind."**
- *Literal:* A narrow stone service staircase; he pulls off the frame and the glass box and
  leaves them on the steps.
- *Analogy:* Like peeling an orange or unwrapping a present: layer after layer.
- *Scale:* A weight scale: frame and glass ~ almost 200 lb vs. painting 18 lb. The scale tips
  hard, the bowl with the frame hits the floor.
- *Personified:* Without her frame, she looks embarrassed, like being caught without clothes;
  her eyes look down.
- *Kid-drawn:* A box, then the box comes off and a little flat square comes out.
- **Pick:** the unwrapping, and the frame lying on the stairs like an empty shell. A frame without
  a painting is a strong image and comes back on line 2.6 and 1.14.
- **Grows into next:** the empty frame on the stairs; we move through it as if through a window.

**1.10 "Now she's just a painting on a thin wooden board. He wraps her in his smock."**
- *Literal:* The thin poplar board; he takes off his white smock and wraps it around her.
- *Analogy:* Like a baby in a blanket, or a burrito.
- *Scale:* She's about the size of a school notebook or a pizza box: compare her with everyday
  objects so the viewer feels how small and thin she is.
- *Personified:* Just before the smock closes, her eyes peek out over the edge.
- *Kid-drawn:* A tiny lady wrapped up in a sheet like a sleeping bag.
- **Pick:** the burrito wrap with her eyes peeking out. This is the cloth device (1.2) entering.
- **Grows into next:** the white smock fills the frame and becomes the white of a big door.

**1.11 "But the door at the bottom of the stairs is locked."**
- *Literal:* He tries the handle; it doesn't move. His key doesn't work.
- *Wacky:* The door grows taller and taller the more he tries, and a huge padlock appears on it.
- *Personified:* The door frowns; the keyhole is its mouth saying "No."
- *Analogy:* Like a game where you reach the last level and the door says "you need the key".
- *Kid-drawn:* A door with an X on it, a man with sweat drops.
- **Pick:** the padlock door that grows, plus sweat drops; tension in one image.
- **Grows into next:** footsteps; a shadow with a wrench appears on the door.

**1.12 "Then a plumber walks by. He thinks Vincenzo is a worker, so he opens the door for him!"**
- *Literal:* A plumber with tools opens it with his key and pliers and holds it politely.
- *Wacky:* The plumber bows like a hotel doorman: "After you, sir." Maybe a red carpet.
- *Personified:* Her eyes from inside the smock go wide: "Seriously?"
- *Analogy:* Like a stranger holding the door for a thief carrying a TV out.
- *Kid-drawn:* Two stick men, one in a white coat, the other holding a key, both smiling.
- **Pick:** the doorman bow and red carpet, with the smock-eyes rolling. The most remembered
  beat of part 1.
- **Grows into next:** daylight pours through the open door and fills the frame.

**1.13 "Vincenzo walks out into the streets of Paris, carrying the Mona Lisa."**
- *Literal:* A morning street, people passing, a man in a vest with a white bundle under his arm.
- *Scale:* Bird's-eye: one tiny white dot moves through a big map of Paris streets.
- *Wacky:* Passers-by: a baker, a lady with a dog, a policeman; each walks by within inches and
  doesn't look.
- *Personified:* Her eyes peek from the bundle at the city; she's never been outside.
- *Kid-drawn:* A map with a dotted line and an X.
- **Pick:** passers-by brushing past, including a policeman tipping his hat. Then up to the
  bird's eye dot vanishing among streets.
- **Grows into next:** the dot disappears; we zoom back down the map into the Louvre roof, next
  day.

**1.14 "The next day, a painter comes to paint her. He finds only four empty hooks."**
- *Literal:* A painter with an easel walks in and stops; on the wall, four empty hooks.
- *Wacky:* He has already painted a copy on his easel and looks between the copy and the empty
  wall; he scratches his head.
- *Scale:* Very close to the hooks: four small things on a big empty wall.
- *Analogy:* Like going to the zoo and the cage is empty with just the sign left.
- *Kid-drawn:* An empty rectangle with four dots and a sad face.
- **Pick:** the painter with his canvas, confused, then close on the four empty hooks (device 1.1).
- **Grows into next:** a guard walks into frame and shrugs.

**1.15 "At first, the guards think the photographers took her for pictures. They didn't."**
- *Literal:* Guards shrug and point to the photography studio.
- *Wacky:* A daydream bubble: she poses for a photo shoot with a huge camera, flash, posing for
  glamour shots. On "They didn't." the bubble pops.
- *Personified:* In the bubble, she strikes model poses.
- *Analogy:* Like assuming your car was towed when actually it was stolen.
- *Kid-drawn:* A box camera with "SNAP!".
- **Pick:** the glamour photo shoot daydream bubble that pops. Funny and then a punchline.
- **Grows into next:** the bubble pops; the drops become a newspaper headline forming.

**1.16 "The Mona Lisa is gone. And no one knows who took her."**
- *Literal:* The empty wall.
- *Personified:* Somewhere in the dark, under a white smock, two eyes look out at us.
- *Wacky:* Police photos on a board, every face a "?".
- *Scale:* The empty wall pulls back until the hole is small in a big city, and the city in a
  world.
- *Kid-drawn:* A "WANTED" poster with a question mark.
- **Pick (cliffhanger):** the empty hooks, then through the gap we go into darkness where two
  eyes peek from under a white cloth.
- **Grows into next part:** the four empty hooks.

### Part 2: the hunt

**2.1 "Last time, a man named Vincenzo walked out of the Louvre with the Mona Lisa."**
- *Literal:* Open on the four empty hooks from the end of part 1. In the empty space on the wall,
  part 1 replays in **rewind**: the bundle walks backwards in through the door, the plumber bows
  backwards, and the hand puts her back on the hooks... then it plays forward again and she's gone.
- *Analogy:* A flipbook in the guard's hands, flicking through part 1's key frames in three seconds.
- *Kid-drawn:* A three-panel comic on the empty wall: hooks / smock bundle / open door.
- *Personified:* The empty hooks themselves "remember": a thought bubble rises from them with her
  face in it.
- **Pick:** rewind-then-forward inside the empty space; the viewer who missed part 1 gets it in
  one look, and we never leave the wall.
- **Grows into next:** the four hooks twist and bend together into one big **"?"**.

**2.2 "This is The Stolen Smile. Part two: the hunt."**
- *Literal:* The title on the empty wall.
- *Wacky:* A detective's magnifying glass swings into frame like a pendulum. Wherever it passes,
  the title words appear under the lens and disappear outside it.
- *Analogy:* A hunting horn sounds and the title rides past like a fox hunt: little paper
  policemen on bicycles chase the letters across the wall.
- *Kid-drawn:* "THE HUNT" with a magnifying glass for the "U".
- **Pick:** the swinging magnifying glass revealing the title.
- **Grows into next:** the lens stops on the floor; through it we see police boots flooding the hall.

**2.3 "The museum closes for a whole week while police search it."**
- *Literal:* Police with lanterns in every gallery, the doors chained, a "Closed" sign.
- *Wacky:* The search is a rummage: a policeman lifts the lid of an Egyptian sarcophagus and the
  mummy shakes its head "not here"; another looks under a statue's skirt and gets slapped by a
  marble hand; a third looks inside a vase and gets his head stuck.
- *Scale:* The Louvre as a doll's house with its front wall swung open: dozens of rooms, a tiny
  policeman in each, all searching at once; a seven-day calendar in the corner tears off a page
  per second.
- *Analogy:* Like tearing the whole house apart for your lost keys: drawers out, cushions off.
- *Personified:* The sleeping Louvre from 1.4 is now wide awake, eyes darting, sweating.
- *Kid-drawn:* A building with every window showing a stick man holding a magnifying glass.
- **Pick:** the doll's-house cross-section with the silly searches inside individual rooms, plus
  the calendar ripping. One frame, lots to look at, and time passes in it.
- **Grows into next:** the last torn calendar page flies out of the window and lands on a printing
  press, where it becomes a front page.

**2.4 "Newspapers all over the world tell the story. Suddenly, everyone knows her name."**
- *Literal:* Printing presses spit out front pages with her face; headlines in French, English,
  German, Spanish.
- *Scale:* A camera flies back from one newspaper to the whole globe: papers fly off Paris like a
  flock of birds and land in London, New York, Buenos Aires, Tokyo. Each landing lights up a dot.
- *Analogy:* Like something going viral: a "share" count under the front page ticking up.
- *Personified:* On every front page, her printed face blinks and looks a little surprised to be
  there.
- *Wacky:* The fame counter (device 1.4) from part 1 clicks so fast it smokes; the monocled art
  lover from 1.2 is pushed aside by a stampede of newspaper readers.
- *Kid-drawn:* A globe with newspapers flapping around it like birds.
- **Pick:** paper birds flying out over the globe and the fame counter going wild. This is the
  series' turning point: she becomes famous *because* she is gone.
- **Grows into next:** one paper bird lands on a lamp-post in Paris and folds open as a song sheet.

**2.5 "People sing songs about her, and sell funny postcards."**
- *Literal:* A cabaret singer on a little stage; a postcard stand on the pavement.
- *Wacky:* The postcards come alive one after another: her waving from a hot-air balloon, her on
  a beach with sunglasses, her riding a bicycle out of the Louvre, "Wish you were here!"
- *Analogy:* The 1911 version of memes: a phone-screen "scroll" made of postcards.
- *Personified:* The music notes from the song flutter round the empty hooks and hang on them like
  little birds.
- *Kid-drawn:* A smiling lady on a postcard with "HELLO FROM SOMEWHERE?".
- **Pick:** the postcard fan, each card a different gag of where she "is". Kids remember the beach
  sunglasses one.
- **Grows into next:** the last postcard shows the empty wall; we push into the card and it is the
  real wall, with a crowd in front of it.

**2.6 "When the museum opens again, crowds line up to stare at an empty wall."**
- *Literal:* A long queue in the gallery; people standing, solemn, in front of four hooks.
- *Scale:* The queue keeps going: out the door, along the Seine, round the block, the camera
  pulling up and up to show it snaking through Paris.
- *Wacky:* People take off their hats and lay flowers under the hooks; one man brings opera
  glasses to look at nothing.
- *Analogy:* Like a long queue at a concert to see the stage after the singer has left.
- *Personified:* The four hooks, embarrassed by the attention, try to look busy.
- *Kid-drawn:* Lots of people looking at a blank square, all with big round eyes.
- **Pick:** the queue that never ends, and the opera glasses aimed at nothing. Absurd and true.
- **Grows into next:** the camera slides down the queue and stops on one face with big ears.

**2.7 "One of them is the famous writer Franz Kafka."**
- *Literal:* A slim young man with dark hair, a high collar and large ears, a bowler hat in his
  hands, looking at the hooks.
- *Wacky:* A little name tag pops up over his head, like a celebrity spotted in a crowd, with a
  camera "flash"; the others in the queue don't notice.
- *Personified:* The hooks notice him: one hook straightens like a fan seeing a star.
- *Kid-drawn:* "FAMOUS WRITER" with an arrow pointing to one man in the crowd.
- *Analogy:* Like spotting a celebrity in the queue for the same empty thing you came to see.
- **Pick:** the "celebrity spotted" name tag and flash, then he writes something in a tiny
  notebook. Keep it light: even famous people came to see nothing.
- **Grows into next:** his pen draws a long line that becomes a detective's line of string on a
  pinboard.

**2.8 "Who did it? Police arrest a famous poet and lock him up for a few days."**
- *Literal:* The detective's pinboard: photos, red string. A poet in a hat is led to a prison cell.
- *Wacky:* The bars of his cell are lines of handwritten poetry, and he leans on them, reading.
- *Analogy:* Like a teacher blaming the kid who was standing nearest the broken window.
- *Personified:* The "?" from 2.1 floats over the pinboard and lands on the poet's photo like a
  target, then hops away.
- *Scale:* A calendar with three or four days in the corner tearing off: "a few days".
- *Kid-drawn:* A man behind bars with a feather pen and a sad face.
- **Pick:** cell bars made of poetry. Instantly tells "poet" and "jail".
- **Grows into next:** the "?" hops along the red string to the next photo.

**2.9 "They even question a young painter named Pablo Picasso."**
- *Literal:* A young Picasso in a striped shirt under a hanging lamp, detectives leaning in.
- *Wacky:* As the questions get harder, his face starts to slide apart into a cubist face
  (eye here, nose there) from nerves, then snaps back.
- *Personified:* The lamp swings like it's suspicious too.
- *Kid-drawn:* A man with a paintbrush and a big striped shirt, sweat drops.
- *Analogy:* Like being questioned because your friend borrowed something from the shop.
- **Pick:** the cubist nervous face. Even people who don't know Picasso get a laugh from the
  faces sliding.
- **Grows into next:** the hanging lamp swings across the frame to show both men side by side.

**2.10 "But neither one stole her. Both are let go."**
- *Literal:* A cell door swings open; the poet and Picasso walk out into daylight.
- *Wacky:* A rubber stamp "NOT HIM" slams on both photos on the pinboard; the "?" from 2.8 is
  left alone on the board, shrugging.
- *Personified:* The red string, embarrassed, droops off the board.
- *Analogy:* Like crossing two names off a guess-who board: both flip down.
- *Kid-drawn:* Two smiley faces and two big crosses.
- **Pick:** the Guess Who board flipping down two faces; one tile left with a blank "?".
- **Grows into next:** the last blank tile is a thumbprint.

**2.11 "The thief left a thumbprint behind. But the police files can't match it."**
- *Literal:* On the abandoned glass box (from the stairwell in 1.9), a single thumbprint; a
  detective dusts it with powder.
- *Scale:* We zoom into the thumbprint until its ridges are a maze the size of a garden, and a tiny
  detective walks through it.
- *Wacky:* The thumbprint itself, with a little face, goes knocking on filing cabinets: "Is this
  me? Is this me?" Every drawer says no.
- *Personified:* The thumbprint shrugs.
- *Kid-drawn:* A thumbprint drawn with a smiley face, crying.
- **Pick:** the zoom into the thumbprint maze; the detective at the centre finds a filing cabinet
  wall and is stuck.
- **Grows into next:** the maze walls straighten into rows of filing drawers.

**2.12 "Back then, police sorted their files by body size, not by fingerprints."**
- *Literal:* Rows of filing drawers labelled by head length, arm span, ear size; a clerk with
  calipers measures a man's ear.
- *Analogy:* Like a library that sorts books by how thick they are instead of their title: you
  have the title, but you can't find the book.
- *Wacky:* The thumbprint tries to fit into the drawers: "Tall", "Medium", "Big ears". It doesn't
  have ears. It doesn't fit anywhere.
- *Scale:* A wall of drawers rising like a skyscraper, the thumbprint tiny at the bottom.
- *Kid-drawn:* Boxes labelled "BIG", "MEDIUM", "SMALL" and a thumbprint going "??".
- **Pick:** the library analogy in one quick image, then the thumbprint that can't find a drawer
  because it has no ears. The fact sticks because it's funny.
- **Grows into next:** one drawer slides out, and its inside is a tiny room: Vincenzo's.

**2.13 "Police even knock on Vincenzo's door. They look around his little room, and leave."**
- *Literal:* A detective in a small room with a bed, a table with a cloth, a trunk, a window.
  Vincenzo watches with a calm face. The detective looks around and leaves.
- *Wacky:* Hide-and-seek: the detective checks under the bed, behind the curtain, inside the
  stove... and turns his back on the table at the exact moment the cloth moves.
- *Personified:* Her eyes peek out from under the tablecloth just behind the detective's back,
  then duck down when he turns (device 1.3).
- *Analogy:* Like a game of hot and cold: the music gets "hot, hotter" as he gets near the table,
  and then he walks off "cold".
- *Kid-drawn:* A house with a door; inside, a painting with an arrow "HERE!!!".
- **Pick:** the hide-and-seek with her eyes peeking behind his back. Every viewer wants to shout.
- **Grows into next:** the door closes; Vincenzo lifts the cloth.

**2.14 "And where is the Mona Lisa? Hidden in that same little room."**
- *Literal:* Vincenzo lifts the cloth; she is there, on the table or in the trunk.
- *Scale:* Pull out of the window: one lit window in a whole building, in a whole street, in all
  of Paris, while every newspaper in the world asks where she is.
- *Personified:* She looks straight at us with a secret, tiny smile, as if we're in on it.
- *Wacky:* The giant "?" from 2.1 is hanging in the sky over Paris, and the camera tilts down from
  it right into this room. The answer was under the question the whole time.
- *Kid-drawn:* A map of Paris with a red X on one tiny house.
- **Pick:** tilt from the huge "?" in the sky down into the one lit window. The answer under the
  question.
- **Grows into next:** the light through the window starts to move across her: time passes.

**2.15 "Days turn into months. Months turn into years."**
- *Literal:* The light through the window sweeps round and round; snow, leaves, rain outside.
- *Analogy:* A flowerpot on the windowsill grows, blooms, wilts and grows again.
- *Wacky:* Vincenzo's moustache grows longer and shorter with each season.
- *Scale:* Split one frame into two places that echo each other: at the Louvre, dust gathers on
  the four empty hooks and a spider builds a web between them (device 1.1); in the room, the
  seasons pass in the window.
- *Kid-drawn:* A calendar where the numbers fly off like leaves.
- **Pick:** the spider web growing between the four hooks. It's the image of "forgotten".
- **Grows into next:** the web glistens and fills the frame like mist.

**2.16 "Many people fear she's gone forever."**
- *Literal:* People sigh at the empty wall; a newspaper headline "Lost forever?".
- *Wacky:* A "Missing" poster on a lamp-post fades in the sun until her face is almost gone.
- *Personified:* The four hooks droop, as if they've given up.
- *Analogy:* Like a lost cat poster still on the pole after years.
- *Kid-drawn:* A poster with "LOST" and a sad face.
- **Pick:** the fading missing poster; her face almost erased, and the smile is the last thing
  left.
- **Grows into next:** a gust of wind tears the poster off; it flies south.

**2.17 "Then, two years later, an art dealer in Florence, Italy, opens a letter."**
- *Literal:* A shop of paintings in Florence; a man at a desk slits an envelope.
- *Scale:* The poster flies over the Alps like a paper plane, over a map, and becomes an envelope
  that lands on a desk in Florence.
- *Wacky:* The envelope has little wings, and it knocks on the door of the shop.
- *Personified:* The Duomo of Florence leans in to read over his shoulder.
- *Kid-drawn:* An envelope with wings over mountains.
- **Pick:** paper-plane poster turns into the envelope over the map. One object, two years, two
  countries.
- **Grows into next:** the letter unfolds until it fills the frame.

**2.18 "The writer says he has the Mona Lisa. And he signs it, Leonardo."**
- *Literal:* Handwriting fills the page; the dealer's eyes widen; the pen strokes of the
  signature.
- *Wacky:* Leonardo da Vinci himself, from his famous self-portrait, pops up in a corner of the
  letter, reads the signature and raises one bushy eyebrow: "Me?".
- *Personified:* The ink of the signature is still wet and drips.
- *Analogy:* Like a ransom note signed "the Queen".
- *Kid-drawn:* A letter with "I HAVE HER. LOVE, LEONARDO" and a heart.
- **Pick (cliffhanger):** the dealer's face over the letter, then close on the wet signature; one
  drop of ink falls.
- **Grows into next part:** the ink drop.

### Part 3: the return

**3.1 "Last time, a letter arrived in Florence, signed Leonardo."**
- *Literal:* The letter on the desk, the signature.
- *Wacky:* The falling ink drop from 2.18 hits the paper and spreads into a map of Florence:
  streets, the river, the Duomo, all in ink.
- *Personified:* Leonardo's eyebrow from 2.18 is still raised.
- *Kid-drawn:* An envelope with "FLORENCE" and a stamp.
- **Pick:** the ink drop becoming Florence. The recap and the new place in one movement.
- **Grows into next:** a ribbon of red silk unwinds across the ink map.

**3.2 "This is The Stolen Smile. Part three: the return."**
- *Literal:* The title over the map.
- *Wacky:* The red silk ribbon writes the title in joined-up letters, like a rhythmic gymnast.
- *Analogy:* Like a gift ribbon: the return as a present being wrapped.
- **Pick:** red silk writing the title (introduces the red silk of 3.6 early).
- **Grows into next:** the ribbon wraps around a stack of bank notes.

**3.3 "The art dealer is named Alfredo. In the letter, the writer asks for a huge pile of money."**
- *Literal:* Alfredo at his desk (suit, moustache), reading the letter.
- *Scale:* The stack of lira notes grows: as high as the desk, the ceiling, then through the
  roof as tall as the Duomo's dome, and a pigeon lands on top.
- *Wacky:* Alfredo's eyebrows rise off his face and float up with the pile.
- *Analogy:* Like a price tag with too many zeros: the zeros keep rolling out onto the floor.
- *Kid-drawn:* A mountain of money with "$$$$$$".
- **Pick:** money pile taller than the Duomo with a pigeon on top.
- **Grows into next:** the pile topples and the notes fall as stairs leading to a hotel door.

**3.4 "Alfredo pretends he wants to buy it. But secretly, he brings a museum expert to a small hotel."**
- *Literal:* Alfredo and a serious man in a suit walk to a small hotel.
- *Wacky:* Alfredo winks at the camera; the expert has a giant magnifying glass hidden badly behind
  his back that sticks out on both sides.
- *Analogy:* Like a TV sting: a "deal", but the cops are behind the curtain.
- *Personified:* The hotel sign flickers, nervous.
- *Kid-drawn:* Two men with a speech bubble "Shhh!".
- **Pick:** the wink and the badly hidden magnifying glass.
- **Grows into next:** climbing the stairs to a door marked 20.

**3.5 "In room twenty, a thin man with a mustache opens a white trunk."**
- *Literal:* A small hotel room, third floor; a thin man in a suit and tie with a moustache; a
  white trunk.
- *Wacky:* The brass "20" on the door is huge and swings like a door knocker.
- *Personified:* The trunk shivers.
- *Analogy:* Like opening a treasure chest at the bottom of the sea.
- *Kid-drawn:* A box with "TOP SECRET" on it.
- **Pick:** the door "20" swings open; we see his face, and the viewer realises who it is before
  the narrator says it (the moustache from part 1).
- **Grows into next:** the lid rises; inside, socks and shirts.

**3.6 "He lifts out a false bottom. Underneath, wrapped in red silk, is the Mona Lisa."**
- *Literal:* Shirts out, then a board lifted, then red silk underneath.
- *Wacky:* The silk is pulled off like a magician's cloth: "Ta-da!" (device 1.2).
- *Personified:* She blinks at the light after two years in the dark, like someone woken up.
- *Analogy:* Like a Russian doll: trunk, then false bottom, then silk, then her.
- *Kid-drawn:* A box, inside another box, inside a lady.
- **Pick:** the Russian-doll layers then the magician's silk pull with her blinking in the light.
- **Grows into next:** a magnifying glass comes down over her face.

**3.7 "The expert checks the tiny cracks in the paint, and a seal on the back. It's the real one!"**
- *Literal:* A loupe over the fine cracks; the panel turned over to a seal.
- *Scale:* We dive into the cracks; they're like the streets of a city from above, then like the
  ridges of a fingerprint.
- *Analogy:* A painting's cracks are its fingerprint: two prints laid over each other match line
  for line.
- *Wacky:* The expert listens to the painting with a stethoscope.
- *Kid-drawn:* A magnifying glass and a big tick.
- **Pick:** the cracks as a fingerprint that matches. It rhymes with the thumbprint of part 2.
- **Grows into next:** the "match" stamp becomes handcuffs.

**3.8 "The police arrest the thief. It's Vincenzo."**
- *Literal:* Police fill the doorway of room 20; handcuffs on the thin man.
- *Wacky:* The big "?" that has hung over the whole of part 2 drops out of the sky, lands on
  his head, and straightens into a "!".
- *Personified:* She gives him a slow side-eye from the trunk: "told you".
- *Analogy:* The last piece of a jigsaw: the pinboard from 2.8 comes back, and his photo clicks
  into the empty centre spot.
- *Scale:* Close on his face, then the glass reflection from 1.6 returns: his face over hers.
  The man who made her glass box, caught by a magnifying glass.
- *Kid-drawn:* A stick man with handcuffs and "GOT YOU!".
- **Pick:** the "?" falling and straightening into "!". It pays off the question that has been
  hanging for a whole part.
- **Grows into next:** the "!" stretches up into a flagpole, and a flag unrolls from it.

**3.9 "Why did he do it? He said the Mona Lisa belonged in Italy. He thought she had been stolen from there."**
- *Literal:* Vincenzo in a courtroom chair, hand on heart, speaking passionately.
- *Analogy:* Like a kid who "rescues" a toy from his cousin's house because he's sure it was his,
  and hugs it.
- *Wacky:* In his imagination (a thought bubble shaped like the boot of Italy), she arrives home
  to a brass band, confetti and a parade, and he's carried on shoulders as a hero.
- *Personified:* She raises one eyebrow in the bubble, not convinced.
- *Scale:* A map of Europe: a dotted arrow from Italy to France labelled "stolen?" drawn by his
  hand in chalk.
- *Kid-drawn:* A boot-shaped country with a heart and a painting in it.
- **Pick:** the hero-parade daydream with her raised eyebrow. His motive and its doubt in one
  picture.
- **Grows into next:** the dotted arrow on the map; a big eraser wipes it out.

**3.10 "But that wasn't true. Leonardo da Vinci brought her to France himself."**
- *Literal:* An old Leonardo with a long white beard crossing the Alps on a mule, the painting
  strapped to his luggage, and arriving at the French king's court.
- *Wacky:* The chalk arrow is rubbed out and redrawn the right way: Leonardo himself grabs the
  chalk out of Vincenzo's hand and draws it, then taps the arrow twice.
- *Analogy:* Like accusing your neighbour of stealing your bike when you sold it to him.
- *Personified:* She nods slightly in agreement, ever so slightly.
- *Scale:* A tiny figure with a big painting crossing huge mountains.
- *Kid-drawn:* An old man with a beard carrying a painting, with a speech bubble "I'm taking her
  to France!".
- **Pick:** Leonardo grabbing the chalk and fixing the arrow; the history correction becomes a
  gag with no extra words needed.
- **Grows into next:** the arrow flips and points back down into Italy, now as a red carpet
  rolling out through Florence, Rome and Milan.

**3.11 "Before she goes home, Italy shows her off. Huge crowds come to see her."**
- *Literal:* Packed galleries in Florence, Rome and Milan; people pressing forward; police
  holding the crowd back.
- *Wacky:* A farewell tour poster like a rock star: "Mona Lisa: Italy Tour 1913. Florence, Rome,
  Milan. One night only!"
- *Analogy:* Like a star singer's farewell concert tour before going home.
- *Scale:* The fame counter (device 1.4) clicks up with every city; the crowds become a sea of
  hats.
- *Personified:* She smiles a little wider (device 1.3, smile meter), enjoying it.
- *Kid-drawn:* A big crowd with hearts above their heads.
- **Pick:** the rock-star tour poster that comes alive into packed rooms. Fun and accurate.
- **Grows into next:** the last city on the poster is crossed out, and "PARIS" writes itself in.

**3.12 "Then, in January 1914, she goes back on her wall in Paris."**
- *Literal:* Back in the Salon Carré: two men lift her onto the wall and hang her on the four iron
  hooks.
- *Wacky:* The spider web between the hooks from 2.15 is gently swept away; the spider packs a
  tiny suitcase and leaves.
- *Personified:* The four hooks wake up, shake off the dust, and reach up eagerly. Each "clink"
  is a happy sound (device 1.1), counted 1, 2, 3, 4 like in part 1.
- *Analogy:* Like a lost puzzle piece that fits back into its gap.
- *Scale:* Through a winter snowy window, the Louvre gets its eyes open again (the "sleeping
  Louvre" from 1.4 now wide awake and smiling).
- *Kid-drawn:* A painting with four dots above and "HOME" in big letters.
- **Pick:** the four clinks of the hooks, mirroring 1.7 exactly. The strongest series-wide
  callback.
- **Grows into next:** the camera turns from her to the gallery doors, which burst open.

**3.13 "In just two days, more than a hundred thousand people come to see her."**
- *Literal:* A flood of people through the doors and up the stairs.
- *Scale:* The crowd fills the room, then the Louvre, then Paris itself; from above it's a sea of
  hats in the shape of a giant smile.
- *Analogy:* Like a sold-out stadium (100,000 is about a big football stadium full) walking
  through one room.
- *Wacky:* The fame counter (device 1.4) can't count fast enough and the little brass numbers pop
  out like popcorn.
- *Personified:* She tries to keep a straight face and fails, just a tiny bit wider.
- *Kid-drawn:* "100,000" written with lots of zeros, and the zeros are faces.
- **Pick:** the counter overflowing into popcorn numbers, and the crowd from above in the shape of
  a smile.
- **Grows into next:** one of the popped number tiles shrinks to a tiny calendar page: "7 months".

**3.14 "Vincenzo spends about seven months in jail."**
- *Literal:* Vincenzo in a plain cell; tally marks on the wall.
- *Wacky:* His tally marks on the wall are drawn as tiny four-hook rows, and he looks at them and
  sighs.
- *Analogy:* Like being grounded, not forever.
- *Personified:* On the wall of his cell hangs a postcard of the Mona Lisa from 2.5. She looks
  at him, not angry, a bit sorry for him.
- *Scale:* His small cell beside a huge, crowded Louvre: two windows side by side, one dark and
  quiet, one bright and full.
- *Kid-drawn:* A man behind bars with "7" in a speech bubble.
- **Pick:** the postcard on his cell wall watching him. It closes their relationship with a
  look.
- **Grows into next:** we push into the postcard and it becomes the real painting again.

**3.15 "And the Mona Lisa? She's more famous than ever."**
- *Literal:* Her face on newspapers, posters, a cinema screen of the time.
- *Wacky:* The monocled art lovers from part 1 (1.2) come back, now crushed at the very back of a
  huge crowd, standing on tiptoe; one lifts his monocle and still can't see.
- *Personified:* Her smile meter reaches its peak.
- *Scale:* The fame counter from part 1, which read "a handful", now reads more digits than fit
  on its face.
- *Kid-drawn:* A lady with a crown and "MOST FAMOUS" written above her.
- **Pick:** the art lovers from 1.2 crushed at the back. A callback that shows the change in one
  glance.
- **Grows into next:** the sepia crowd colours into today's clothes; phones rise.

**3.16 "Today, millions of people visit her every year."**
- *Literal:* Today's Louvre: a huge room, a long rope line, and phones held high in front of her
  behind glass.
- *Scale:* A year-long time-lapse where the crowd flows past her like a river and never stops.
- *Analogy:* As many visitors in a year as the whole population of a big city.
- *Wacky:* The same frame we opened part 1 with (line 1.1), with the sea of phones.
- *Personified:* In the sea of flashes, she glances to the side at an empty spot by the door,
  as if remembering the small man in the white smock.
- *Kid-drawn:* Tiny people everywhere and one painting.
- **Pick:** the sea of phones from 1.1 returns, but this time the viewer knows why. The series
  closes its loop.
- **Grows into next:** the camera pushes towards her face through the flashes.

**3.17 "So the man who stole her smile helped make it the most famous smile in the world."**
- *Literal:* Close on her smile.
- *Wacky:* Vincenzo, small, in his white smock, appears in the crowd for a moment as a ghostly
  sepia figure, tips his cap, and is gone.
- *Personified:* Her smile widens by a hair: the last turn of the smile meter. She knows.
- *Scale:* The camera pushes into the smile until its curve is the curve of the Seine, the
  same curve that opened part 1 (1.3); the loop closes.
- *Analogy:* Like a thief who gets caught but accidentally makes the thing he stole priceless.
- *Kid-drawn:* A big smile with a little stick man in a white coat sitting on it.
- **Pick:** Vincenzo's sepia ghost in today's crowd, then the push into the smile that becomes the
  Seine. The single point of focus of the whole series is the smile, and it's where we end.

## 4. Notes for the motion (from the creator's rules)
- **Something must change in every frame.** Avoid a "board with arrows". Every line above has a
  physical change (a cloth moves, a crowd grows, an object transforms), not only labels
  appearing.
- **One point of focus that carries over:** the **Grows into next** lines are the
  shot list for the single continuous camera move. The recurring objects (hooks, cloth, "?",
  fame counter, smile curve) are what makes those handoffs feel planned.
- **No black void:** every look above has a warm surface (paper, newsprint, painted walls) and a
  place, so it reads as a film and not a slide.
- **Facts to keep straight in the pictures** (from the research): four iron hooks; frame and
  glass left on the service stairs; a plumber opened the locked door; she's a thin poplar board,
  ~77 x 53 cm; Picasso was questioned, not jailed; the file problem was body-measurement filing;
  the white trunk with a false bottom and red silk in room 20 in Florence; cracks and a seal
  proved she was real; Leonardo took her to France himself; back on the wall in January 1914;
  about seven months in jail.
